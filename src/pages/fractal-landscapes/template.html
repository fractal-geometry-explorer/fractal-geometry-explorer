Plates C9–C15Landscapes That Never Were

The colour signature's landscapes are not photographs, and they are not processed from real terrain. Each one is a fractional Brown surface: a random relief whose only inputs are a dimension D, a seed, and a sea level. Richard F. Voss computed the originals; these figures rebuild the same models, so every mountain here is new, but its statistics are the book's.

Plate C11

Gaussian hills that never were

Brown relief · surface D = 3 − H · coastline D = 2 − H

Mandelbrot asks for the simplest possible model of relief: the altitude difference between any two points should be Gaussian, and the landscape should have no preferred scale. The only family that satisfies both is the Brown and fractional Brown surfaces, and the only parameter left free is the fractal dimension D between 2 and 3. At D = 2 the relief is smooth; raise D and it grows corrugated, passes through believable mountains, and finally approaches a space-filling roughness. The altitude is computed on a square grid, a flat sea is flooded in, and "a semblance of roundness is injected by rolling this relief's flat base surface around a cylinder", lit from 60° over the left — all as the caption describes.

A random plate is matched statistically, not stroke for stroke: the print's exact mountains came from Voss's seed, which the book doesn't give. The "as printed" preset sets the model's parameters and a seed chosen so the scene composes like the plate — snow on a high peak at the right, a lake in the middle ground. The caption's test is worth trying here: lower the vertical scale and the Sierras turn into rolling farmland, though D never changed.

Plate C13

Non-Gaussian hills that never were

the same D throughout · vertical scale varies with altitude: h → h^γ above the valley floor

In a Gaussian landscape the valley bottoms are exactly as rough as the mountain tops, while real valleys are smoother — flooding the valleys with lakes and snow was what hid this defect in the other plates. Plate C13 shows Mandelbrot's simplest fix: keep the same D everywhere, but let the vertical scale grow with the altitude above the valley floor. Raising the altitude to the third power gives the gentle valley and sudden peaks of the top panel; a power below 1 instead flattens the tops into the mesa and canyon of the bottom panel.

Plate C15

Fractal islands that never were, seen from the zenith

top archipelago: Brown, D = 5/2, coastlines D = 3/2 · bottom: persistent, D = 2.200, coastlines D = 1.200

To make islands, the caption says, "we center the relief around a maximum, and omit to plot the altitudes below a certain reference level taken as 0". Both archipelagos here use the same seed, so the same underlying randomness wears two dimensions. The top one is an ordinary Brown relief, D = 5/2: a poor model of Earth, too irregular in its detail, its coastline dimension 3/2 well above what maps show. The bottom one is a persistent fractional Brown relief of D = 2.200, whose coastlines take the sensible dimension 1.200 — Mandelbrot notes its resemblance to Hawaii is "better than deserved". The clear-cut ridges appear although the mechanism is isotropic: nothing in the model prefers any direction.

Plate C9

Planetrise over Labelgraph Hill

souvenir from a space mission that never was · two models of planetary relief in one frame

The plate combines Mandelbrot's two theories of planetary surfaces. The distant planet is a Brown relief on the sphere, built by random great-circle faults: each fault lifts one hemisphere by a tiny step, and a few hundred of them leave a relief whose coastline — a Brownian Pangaea — has dimension 3/2. Oceans fill the low side, snow the peaks, and "the sky remains completely cloudless — to put it mildly, a rough approximation". The foreground is Labelgraph Hill, a fractional Brown relief whose altitudes are cubed, the trick of Plate C13, lit low from the left. The hill is named in memory of "lblgraph", the ill-mannered heap of IBM graphics programs that drew the book's plates. R.I.P.

This is the loosest reconstruction on the page, and the caption warns that even the original cannot be duplicated without "exactly the same computer equipment". The two models are the book's; the composition — globe high at the left, hill across the foreground — follows the plate, and the colours are chosen after it. The crater-pocked texture of the printed foreground comes from Voss's rendering and is not reproduced by the cubed-altitude model alone. The planet model itself is the subject of Plates 269–271 in Chapter 28.
