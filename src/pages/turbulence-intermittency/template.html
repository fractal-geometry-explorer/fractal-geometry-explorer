Chapter 10Turbulence and Intermittency

Wind comes in gusts, the gusts in smaller gusts, down to scales no airplane feels — turbulent dissipation concentrates on a thinning set, a phenomenon called intermittency. Mandelbrot's claim: that set is a fractal, with a dimension measurements put around 2.5 to 2.6, and even defining "turbulent" requires one. This chapter prints no plates, so every figure here is this page's own illustration of its argument, built from the curdling of chapter 8 carried into space.

Gusts within gusts: the record a probe reads

base 9, N gusts per eddy · d = log N / log 9 · in space, D = 2 + d

In the Reynolds pipe, turbulence is all or nothing, and no geometry is called for. In the atmosphere it is not: "certain regions are marked by very high dissipation, while other regions seem by contrast nearly free of dissipation." A large airplane is shaken every so often; a smaller airplane feels each of those shocks as a burst of weaker shocks with calm stretches between, and a finer probe splits the bursts again. Each stage of refinement sharpens the turbulence onto a smaller fraction of the record. The model: curdle the record as in chapter 8, keeping N ninths of every gusty stretch.

Curdling and fractally homogeneous turbulence

base 3: keep N of 27 subeddies · D = log N / log 3 · density × 27/N per stage

Papers on intermittency speak of "blobs, rods, slabs, and ribbons" — shapes that fail to be self-similar. Mandelbrot instead lets the whole and its details come from one process: split each eddy into 27 subeddies of one third its side, and let the dissipation curdle into N of them. After m stages it sits, uniformly, on Nm of the 27m cells; continued without end, the limit has dimension D = log N/log 3, and he names it fractally homogeneous turbulence. Keeping all 27 gives D = 3, G. I. Taylor's homogeneous turbulence: curdling allows the classic case, and the novel possibility D < 3.

Pages 103–104

Why the probes say D > 2

a line meets a fractal of dimension D > 2 with positive probability, and almost never one with D < 2

Run a straight probe — a hot-wire record, an airplane's path — through curdled turbulence. Inside each turbulent eddy the probe crosses a column of 3 subeddies, and each stays turbulent with probability 3D−3; so along the probe the gusts breed like a family tree with 3D−2 children per generation on average. When D < 2 the family dies out: almost every probe eventually reads laminar. When D > 2 it survives forever with positive probability. Probes hit turbulence constantly — an experiment "repeated constantly, with the possible outcomes reduced to an alternative between never and often" — so the support of dissipation must satisfy D > 2.

When D > 2 the trace left on the probe is itself a dust of dimension D − 2, which is how the record of section 1 inherits d = D − 2.

Pages 104–105

The spectrum (in)equality

⟨[v(x) − v(x+r)]²⟩ = |r|2/3 + B · B = (3 − D)/3 · D = 3 gives Kolmogorov's 2/3

Intermittency leaves a fingerprint on the spectrum. In fractally homogeneous turbulence the velocity difference across a distance r satisfies ⟨[v(x) − v(x+r)]²⟩ = |r|2/3 + B with B = (3 − D)/3 — stated by Mandelbrot in 1967, in the notation θ = D − 2. Taylor homogeneous turbulence has D = 3, B vanishes, and the classic Kolmogorov exponent 2/3 is left standing; concentrating the dissipation (D < 3) steepens the exponent. The more general weighted curdling of chapter 23 gives the inequality B ≤ (3 − D)/3 instead.

The formulas are the chapter's; the charts are this page's way of drawing them. The β-model of Frisch, Nelkin & Sulem (1978) grafts a dynamical vocabulary onto this same geometry — the chapter notes its mathematics and conclusions are identical. Measurements put D around 2.5 to 2.6, "but probably below 2.66", so the measured exponent sits a little above Kolmogorov's 2/3 — close enough that the 1941 theory predicted spectra so well while resting on an assumption, uniform dissipation, that the wind's gusts plainly violate.
