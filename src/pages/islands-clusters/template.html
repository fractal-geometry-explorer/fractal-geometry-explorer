Chapter 13Islands, Clusters and Percolation

How many islands surround Britain's coast? When a Koch generator is allowed to split into disconnected pieces, each stage seeds new islands, and their sizes follow the empirical law Korčak found in atlases: the number of islands larger than a is proportional to a−D/2. The same diameter–number relation governs clusters made by curdling and by Peano curves with controlled self-contacts, and it gives a geometric language for a central problem of physics: percolation. A Koch continent and its islands

N = 16, r = 1/8 · D = log 16/log 8 = 4/3 · each coastline Dc = log 10/log 8 ≈ 1.1073

The generator splits into two parts. A connected broken line of Nc links joins 0 to 1: the coastline generator. The remaining N − Nc links form a closed loop that seeds a new island at every stage: the island generator. Sub-islands always stay on the sea side — left of the coastline going from 0 to 1, left of the island loop going clockwise. The limit has two dimensions: D = log N/log(1/r) for all coastlines lumped together, Dc = log Nc/log(1/r) for each one alone. In the second generator the coastline is straight: its islands are plain rectangles, and D measures fragmentation alone, not wiggliness.

Figure, page 121

Islands and lakes, nested without bound

N = 18, r = 1/6 · D = log 18/log 6 ≈ 1.6131 · every coastline is rectifiable: Dc = 1

Relieve the island generator from the requirement of staying to the left. Placed to the right of the coastline it digs lakes instead of raising islands, and one generator may carry both. This one adds, to a straight six-link coastline, one island loop above and one lake loop below. Initiated by a square, it nests islands within lakes within islands without bound: the final fractal is a σ-loop. The land never reaches a continent — extrapolated outward, the biggest island is almost surely found to lie off a bigger island's shore, and so on ad infinitum.

Figure, page 124

Nonlumped curdling clusters

Generator A: N = 12 of b² = 16 cells · D = log 12/log 4 ≈ 1.7925

Cantor curdling carried to the plane: keep N of the b × b cells, then curdle each kept cell in turn. The limit has zero area, and its topology is set entirely by which cells the generator keeps. A keeps a ring and stays connected — a Sierpiński carpet relative, met again in Chapter 14. F keeps four corners and crumbles to dust. B, C and E split into preclusters that thin and wiggle forever: B's largest piece is a straight interval, C's a fractal tree with Dc = log 7/log 4, and E's F-shaped pieces keep splitting, so its clusters, too, end as intervals.

Figure, page 125

Cross-lumped curdling clusters

Left generator: N = 5 of 9 cells · D = log 5/log 3 ≈ 1.4650

Now let kept cells in different cells of the coarser lattice touch: each newborn precluster "cross lumps" contributions from several of them. Every contact cluster then combines an integer number of copies of itself at scale 1/b with many lumped-in copies at smaller scales 1/bm, so the cluster dimension solves Σ amxm = 1 with x = b−Dc — and bDc need not be an integer. The diameter–number relation Nr(Λ > λ) ∝ λ−D still holds for small λ.

Figure, page 126

Knotted Peano monsters, tamed

N = 17, r = 1/√17 · D = 2 · one self-contact per copy of the generator

A plane-filling collection of clusters (D = 2) cannot be made by curdling. Mandelbrot's alternative is a Peano curve whose teragons never cross themselves but touch themselves at carefully controlled points. Each point of self-contact "knots off" an open precluster; chunks of it are knotted away in turn, and what survives thins down to a highly ramified curve — a contact cluster whose diameter is fixed at birth, with the familiar distribution Nr(Λ > λ) ∝ λ−2. These clusters are limits not of curves but of the open components of a curve's complement.

Pages 126–130

Bernoulli percolation

Bond percolation on the square lattice · pcrit = ½ · critical clusters Dc ≈ 1.89 (for E = 2)

Build a lattice of sticks, each independently copper with probability p and insulating vinyl otherwise. Maximal connected sets of copper sticks are copper clusters; when one joins top to bottom, current flows and the lattice percolates. Hammersley's remarkable finding is a threshold: as the lattice grows, percolation becomes almost certain for p > pcrit and almost impossible for p < pcrit — and for bonds on the square lattice pcrit = ½ exactly. At the threshold the clusters are "all skin and no flesh", ramified and stringy at every scale. Mandelbrot's conjecture, since accepted: critical clusters are fractal σ-curves with D = 2 and 1 < Dc < D.

Matching percolation's exponents to the σ-cluster geometry gives Dc = E/(τ−1) = E/(1+δ−1) and Dc = 1 + (E−η)/2; for E = 2 these agree at Dc ≈ 1.89. Early simulations gave the biased estimate ≈ 1.77 — close to the mass exponent Q = 2Dc − D, which briefly misled several authors, Mandelbrot included, into calling Q a dimension.
