Plate 255Fractional Brown Trails

This chapter marks a turning point of the book: instead of building a fractal stage by stage, Mandelbrot declares the properties he wants — Gaussian, stationary, scaling — and then looks for a process that has them. The answer is fractional Brownian motion, invented to explain why wet and dry years on the Nile cluster into long floods and droughts. One parameter H tunes the persistence, and the same random seed drawn at two values of H gives the two trails of Plate 255.

Fractional Brown trails

Coordinates: two independent fractional Brown functions of exponent H · trail D = 1/H · printed: H = 0.9000, D ≈ 1.1111 (left) and H = 0.7000, D ≈ 1.4285 (right)

A trail of fractional Brownian motion: each coordinate is an independent fractional Brown function of the same exponent H, so the walker's displacement over a time Δt grows like ΔtH instead of the ordinary √Δt. With H = 0.9 (left figure) the trend persists strongly in whatever direction it has embarked on, and the small convolutions are barely visible. The right figure runs the same program on the same pseudo-random seed with H = 0.7: the overall shape stays recognizable, but the smaller convolutions grow until they crowd the view.

A random plate: the book's trails came from Mandelbrot & Wallis's 1969 computer program and their seed, which the book does not record, so these trails match the plate statistically, not stroke for stroke. The noise here is exact fractional Gaussian noise (circulant embedding), not the periodic fast-Fourier shortcut the chapter warns about — the covariance is exact at every lag. As in the plate, moving H keeps the same seed, so the trail deforms coherently instead of being redrawn. For H below ½ the motion turns antipersistent and the trail law D = 1/H saturates at the plane's D = 2.

River discharges and the Joseph Effect

Graph of BH(t): D = 2 − H · its zeroset: D = 1 − H · increments' variance over Δt: |Δt|²ᴴ

Mandelbrot reads the stories of Joseph's seven fat and seven lean years, and of Noah's flood, as parables about rivers: wet and dry years cluster into long wet periods and droughts. Hurst found that real rivers are extraordinarily persistent — for the Nile, H ≈ 0.9. Here the lower curve plays the yearly discharges (fractional Gaussian noise) and the upper curve their running sum, the cumulated discharge BH(t). Slide H and watch persistence being injected into the very same sequence of chance: cycles and trends appear that no mechanism put there.

At H = ½ the increments are independent — white Gaussian noise, the Rhine, a river with "no Joseph legend". For H > ½ the persistence extends forever: nearly every sample shows a few slow, nonperiodic cycles and a trend, whichever its duration, and none of it can be extrapolated. Mandelbrot aims this at economists, who like to decompose data into trend, cycles and noise: in fBm the trend and the cycles are the noise, and signify nothing. Below ½ the noise turns antipersistent, constantly turning back on itself.

Chapter 27 concept

Hurst's R/S, scaling noises and 1/f

Hurst: R(d)/S(d) ∝ dᴴ · spectral density of the increments: ∝ 1/f^β with β = 2H − 1

Hurst measured rivers with the rescaled range: over each span of d years, R(d) is the reservoir capacity ideal performance would have needed — the range of the cumulated, detrended discharge — and S(d) the standard deviation. White noise and every textbook process give R/S ∝ √d. The Nile's records instead gave R/S ∝ d0.9. The diagram plots R/S for the simulated discharges on doubly logarithmic paper, one dot per block of d years; the fitted slope recovers H.

A noise is scaling when speeding the tape up only calls for turning the volume: fractional Gaussian noise is one, with spectral density ∝ 1/f^β, β = 2H − 1. Near H = 1 that is the famous "1/f noise". White noise is the flat case β = 0, H = ½. Mandelbrot proved 0 < H < 1 is forced: Hurst's measured slopes near 0.7–0.9 are a symptom of scaling, persistence with no time scale and no characteristic cycle.
