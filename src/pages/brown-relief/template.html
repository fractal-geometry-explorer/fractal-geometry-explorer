The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 28, "Relief and Coastlines" · pages 256–271

Plates 264–271Brown Relief and Coastlines

Mountains like the Alps, Mandelbrot argues, are usefully modeled by fractal surfaces ruled by Brownian chance — and their zerosets, the points at ocean level, at last give a sensible model of coastlines. The ordinary Brown relief has surface dimension D = 5/2 and coastlines of D = 3/2, too rough for most of Earth; its fractional variants reach any dimension the data ask for. Every figure here is a fresh simulation, computed from the chapter's rules with the seed on display.

Plates 264 and 265

Brown lake landscapes, ordinary and fractional

surface D = 3 − H, from ≈ 2.1 to 5/2 · every vertical cut is a (fractional) Brown line-to-line function

The top of Plate 265 is a fractional Brown relief of dimension fairly close to 2 — Mandelbrot's model of Earth's landscape. The other figures extrapolate it to higher D, ending at the top of Plate 264 with the ordinary Brown relief, "definitely rougher than most of Earth's relief": a beautiful long jump forward, but a crude model, begging to be returned to the bench. As in the book, the altitude is computed on a square grid, lit from 60° over the left, and seen from 25° over the base level; water fills every hollow below sea level.

Plates 266 and 267

Brown coastlines, and island "strings"

coastline D = surface D − 1 · 8/3 → 5/3 · 7/3 → 4/3 · 5/2 → 3/2

Slice a fractional Brown relief at sea level and fill the land black. These plates underline a newly discovered effect: when the relief's D reaches and exceeds 2.5, the ocean tends more and more to split into roundish separate "seas", each with a sharp individuality, while the islands come in "strings". The samples look anisotropic — yet the generating mechanism is perfectly isotropic. The coastline of Plate 270's kind was the first curve Mandelbrot had met that avoids itself, has a clear fractal dimension above 1, and is isotropic.

Plate 268

Contour lines in fractional Brown landscapes

same program and seed · top D ≈ 1.3333, bottom D ≈ 1.1667 · the side of a huge mountain

Here the relief has a strong overall slope — we see the side of a huge mountain, not its summit — and a few of its contour lines are drawn, the bold one being the coastline. Both figures use the same program and the same seed, only H changes. The curves seem much less "rugged" than Plate 267's of the same D: with a systematic slope the coastline differs from a straight line only by mild irregularities. Comparing them shows the wide margin left for the interplay between irregularity and fragmentation even after D is fixed.

Plate 269, and C9 (top)

Brownian Pangaea

Lévy's Brown sphere-to-line function Bo(P) · coastline D = 3/2 · three quarters of the area under water

Let the base of the relief be a sphere. Lévy's Brown function from the sphere's points to altitudes was implemented on a computer here for the first time; sea level is adjusted so three quarters of the total area is under water, and the result is shown on a Hammer map, the projection favored by students of Wegener's continental drift. Like the primeval Pangaea, it is a blob of land dented by broad sinuses — on the sphere the Brownian rules involve a strong positive correlation below 60° and a strong negative one between antipodal points, so a big hill at P answers a big hole at its antipode.

Bo(P) is built here by superimposing random great-circle faults — a plateau breaks along a random great circle, one side steps up and the other down — which converges to Lévy's function, the integral of white Gaussian noise over the hemisphere centred at P. The book says the real Pangaea is drawn with D ≈ 1.2 in geology books, while this model forces D = 3/2; only the degrees of wiggliness, not the local detail, are expected to resemble it. The graticule spacing was matched to the plate by eye.

Plate 270

The first known examples of Brown coastlines

top to bottom: D near 1 · D = 1.3000 · D = 3/2 · D closer to 2

Mandelbrot's claim that fractional Brown functions are reasonable models of Earth's relief was originally founded on these four model coastlines, a sentimental carry-over from his 1975 French Essay. When D is near 1, the coastline is too straight to be realistic. At D = 1.3000 it "strongly reminds us of the real Atlas" — he finds echos of Africa, South America in mirror image, and Greenland. At D = 3/2 the guessing game is harder; closer to 2 it becomes specialized (Minnesota? Finland?), then impossible. Finer graphics favor D ≈ 1.2.

Plate 271

Fractional Brown islands, emerging from the sea

surface D = 2.3000 · one island, six sea levels · drawn as a raster, as on the 1976 CRT

Views of an island with varying sea level, from Mandelbrot 1975w — part of the first sequence of fractional Brown islands ever drawn, filmed in 1976 as the island emerged from the sea. The original was photographed from a cathode ray tube, and the raster lines make the sea seem to shimmer toward the horizon. The dimension, D = 2.3, is surprisingly high: early graphics could not show small detail, so the drawn landscapes seemed smoother than the D fed into the program, and D was raised beyond the evidence to compensate.

The six sea levels are not stated in the book; here they fall so the land grows from a few specks to near half the sheet, matching the print by eye.
