The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 34, "Texture: Gaps and Lacunarity; Cirri and Succolarity" · pages 310–318

Plate 318Carpets' Lacunarity

Dimension does not say everything about how a fractal looks. Maps of real galaxies are fairly even; Mandelbrot's early models of them, with the very same D, were riddled with immense voids. Chapter 34 names what differs — lacunarity, from the Latin for gap — and its one plate makes the point with two Sierpiński carpets of identical dimension and utterly different texture. Plate 318

Two carpets, one dimension

b = 1/r = 7, N = 40 · D = log 40/log 7 ≈ 1.8957 for both · only the tremas' arrangement differs

Divide a square into 7 × 7 cells and remove 9 of them — as one 3 × 3 block in the middle, or as 9 separate cells spread out between the rest. Either way N = 40 cells survive, so repeating the cut inside every kept cell leaves a carpet of the same dimension, D = log 40/log 7. "Clearly, D being the same in both cases is not obvious": the left carpet gives the impression of definitely larger gaps. It is the more lacunar one — and the chapter's measures below bear the impression out.

The caption also notes D ≈ 1.8957 is remarkably close to the dimension of Bernoulli percolation — a resemblance it calls misleading, the topologies being very different.

Pages 313–314

Cut along the middle: two Cantor dusts

both mid lines: N = 4, r = 1/7 · D = log 4/log 7 ≈ 0.7124 · clumped against spread-out pieces

Lacunarity "makes sense on the line", so the chapter reads it off the carpets' horizontal mid sections. Both are Cantor dusts keeping N = 4 pieces of length r = 1/7 at every stage — but the left carpet's pieces sit in two clumps at the ends, leaving one big central gap, while the right carpet's are separated by three equal gaps. At one extreme a dust with N ≫ 1 "mimics" a few points, D = 0; at the other, a full interval, D = 1. The true D never budges.

The book names these mid sections but draws them only inside the carpets, so the bar-by-stages layout here follows Plate 80's convention. Masses are those of the limit dust (each kept piece carries 1/4 of its parent's mass), not of the drawn stage. The book's example window has width 2R = 2/7: on the clumped dust its mass ranges from 0 to 1/2, on the spread-out dust it stays near the mean — the chapter's first signature of lacunarity.

Pages 314–316

Measuring lacunarity

the gap census Nr(U > u) ≈ F·u−D · the mass in a window W = M(2R) · lacunarity ⟨(W/⟨W⟩−1)²⟩

Up to now only the exponent in prefactor × (quantity)exponent mattered; "the study of texture forces us to extend our attention to the prefactor." Chapter 8's census of gaps longer than u steps along F·u−D for both dusts — same slope, different height. And where no recursion is available (a random dust, say), the chapter weighs windows instead: how far does the mass W in a window stray from its mean? Low lacunarity, small spread; high lacunarity, large.

The book states these measures in prose and draws no chart, so both figures here are this page's illustrations of the text. The census prefactor is fitted through the stairs' corners; the mass histogram samples 4,000 window positions and keeps, as the chapter prescribes, only the windows that hit the dust. One exact curiosity: at the book's width 2R = 2/7 the spread-out dust weighs precisely 1/4 wherever the window sits — this generator deals mass perfectly evenly at that scale, so its lacunarity measure is 0 there; narrower windows restore a small spread. The chapter's other texture notion, succolarity — "to almost flow through", for fractals that nearly include percolating filaments — is raised but left unmeasured: "beyond the present elementary discussion." Chapter 35 controls both through tremas.
