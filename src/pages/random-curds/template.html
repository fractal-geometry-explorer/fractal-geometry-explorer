The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 23, "Random curds: contact clusters and fractal percolation" · pages 210–223

Plates 218–223Random Curds

Curdling, the grid cascade that built the Cantor dust, turns random here: at each stage every surviving cell of a base-5 grid keeps a few of its subcells, chosen by chance. Five cubes of 125 give Hoyle's D = 1 model of galaxy clusters; keep most of the cells instead and the curd models turbulent dissipation, so dense that it splits into contact clusters, and the question becomes whether one of them percolates from one side of the grid to the other. Plates 218 and 219

Hoyle's model by random curdling in a grid

E = 3, base b = 5 · each cube collapses into N = 5 of its 125 subcubes · D = log 5/log 5 = 1

In Hoyle's model a very low density gas cloud collapses repeatedly: into clusters of galaxies, then galaxies, and so on. The book implements it in the simplest way. An initiator cube of side 1 is divided into 5³ = 125 subcubes, and the matter it holds collapses into 5 of them, chosen at random; then each survivor collapses in turn. Every stage multiplies the density by 25 and reduces the dimension toward D = 1. What is drawn is the projection of the cubes on a face of the initiator, since a dust this sparse leaves space essentially transparent.

Plates 220 through 223

Novikov–Stewart curds, followed by percolation

plane grid, b = 5 · each eddy keeps N of its 25 subeddies · D = log N/log 5, a section of a spatial curd of dimension D + 1

Novikov & Stewart (1964) proposed that turbulent dissipation curdles: each cascade stage takes the precurd and curdles it further into N pieces smaller in the ratio r = 1/5. In turbulence D > 2, so a plane cross section — which subtracts 1 from the dimension, by the rule that codimensions add — is what the plates show. The whey is gray. The curd splits into contact clusters: the connected pieces touching both the top and the bottom of the grid are the percolating ones, left white, and all the others are black.

Pages 210–212, concept

Canonical curdling and the Bienaymé effect

each subinterval survives a coin toss of probability p · ⟨N⟩ = pb · D* = log ⟨N⟩/log b

The chapter's cleanest definition of random curdling needs only coin tossing. Divide an interval into b subintervals; each survives with probability p, independently of the rest, and the cascade resumes inside every survivor. The number of children follows a birth-and-death process, and Irénée Bienaymé saw the fundamental alternative in 1845: when the expected count ⟨N⟩ = pb is 1 or less, the family line almost surely dies out and the cascade leaves the empty set; when ⟨N⟩ > 1, it survives forever with positive probability and yields a random dust of dimension D* = log ⟨N⟩/log b. The threshold ⟨N⟩ = 1 is exactly D* = 0.

The chance of dying out is the smallest root of q = (1 − p + pq)b, computed here by iteration. Below the critical p = 1/b it is 1, and it falls steeply above. The spatial plates above use the constrained variant, where the count N is fixed and only the choice of cells is random; the book notes both give the same dimensions and the same section rules. Rows whose slugs fall under a pixel deposit their true ink coverage per pixel, so thin rows dim instead of banding.
