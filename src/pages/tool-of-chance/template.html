Part VII opens the Essay's second half: why the fractal models now turn random. A coastline's shaping forces are unrecorded and unrecoverable, so statistics is the only honest description — and chance slots neatly into the book's recursions as a controller feeding choices to a processor, with the seed as a page number in a grand portfolio of shapes. The controller, the seed and the grand portfolio

one processor (Plate 46's generator, D = log 3/log √7 ≈ 1.1292) · many control sequences

The Koch loop is a processor program: each run adds fresh detail to what the last run drew. The caption of Plate 46 observes that its generator can be used straight (S) or flipped (F) — so before each loop a controller must say which. Every control sequence is a different curve, all of the same dimension: the loop defines not one fractal but a denumerable family. Replace the tape by a pseudo-random generator and the only parameter of chance left is its input, the seed — a page number in a virtual "grand portfolio", every page one shape, most pages alike in essentials and different in detail.

Chapter 21, pages 203–204

Nonconstrained and self-constrained chance

closed 2n-step lattice polygons: easy · self-avoiding ones: "continues to elude the best minds"

Merely to invoke chance is an empty gesture: vary a coastline's parts at random in shape, size and order, and the pieces will not fit together. The practical divide is whether the controller can run its loops without inspecting their outcome. Lattice polygons of 2n sides, self-intersections allowed, are nonconstrained — easy combinatorics, generated in one pass. Coastlines must not cross themselves, and that single geometric demand makes the chance self-constrained: even counting the self-avoiding polygons is an unsolved problem, and the Essay dodges such chance everywhere but chapter 36.

The sampler pairs each of n random steps with its opposite and shuffles, which closes the loop by construction (a simple closed-walk sampler, not the uniform law on all closed polygons — uniformity is part of what makes the counting hard). The self-avoiding demand is met by brute rejection, and the acceptance chart shows the price: it falls roughly geometrically with n, and past a few dozen sides honest sampling needs the specialist algorithms of lattice physics.

Hyperbolic laws, and a random set's typical D

Two more of the chapter's threads live on other pages. The counting law Nr(U > u) ∝ u⁻ᴰ of chapters 6, 13 and 14 here turns into the probabilist's hyperbolic distribution Pr(U > u) ∝ u⁻ᴰ, star of most remaining chapters — its paradoxes and robustness are drawn on Scaling Without Geometry. And dimension itself needs care once sets are random: in the portfolio each page has its own D, but outside a batch of defective seeds of zero total probability, all pages share one almost sure D. Averaging the sets instead of their dimensions is the trap: the average of all symmetric random walks sits still at its starting point, D = 0, while almost every walk has D = 2 (chapter 25). Likewise a mass exponent M(R) ∝ RQ need not be a dimension — chapter 13's warning, repeated here.
