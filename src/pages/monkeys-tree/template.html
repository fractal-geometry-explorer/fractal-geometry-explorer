A monkeys tree

6·(1/3)D + 5·(1/(3√3))D = 1 · D ≈ 1.8687 · the book draws level 3, 1,331 segments

The plate shades everything on the left of the curve as you walk it from start to finish, then closes the shape with a solid band underneath. Where the curve dips below its starting line, white hooks bite into the band. The book's drawing is level 3, with 1,331 segments, and its corners are drawn as smooth curls — set Arc rounding and White channels to zero to see the exact polygon underneath. Every level multiplies the curve's length by 2 + 5/(3√3) ≈ 2.962, so the length grows without limit while the outline barely moves: the coastline paradox from the same chapter.

The curls are a reconstruction, fitted to a scan of the plate: each segment is redrawn as a biarc — two circular arcs through its endpoints whose tangents match where they meet — and at every vertex the neighbouring biarcs share one tangent, so the outline is a single chain of circles with no kinks. The tangent at a vertex splits the turn there, leaning slightly toward the longer neighbouring segment; that rule, a band 0.295 of the base deep, and a hairline white channel gave the closest overlap with the scan.

The generator

11 segments: six of 1/3 and five of 1/(3√3), along multiples of 30°

Start with a straight segment and replace it with an 11-step zigzag: six steps a third as long as the segment and five steps 1/(3√3) as long, all pointing along multiples of 30°. Then replace each of those steps with a smaller copy of the whole zigzag, and keep going. Each copy can go down four ways: as drawn, mirrored, reversed, or reversed and mirrored. Mandelbrot's arrangement is one of 411, about 4.2 million, and it keeps the curve from ever crossing itself. Most others tangle within two levels.

Each blue sketch shows the smaller copy that replaces that segment at the next level. Hover a segment to find its part of the plate.

With pieces of two sizes, D makes the scaled pieces add to one whole:
6·(1/3)D + 5·(1/(3√3))D = 1  →  D = 1.86873

The arrangement was decoded from the book's second-stage drawing; the self-avoidance badge above checks levels 2 and 3 of whatever arrangement is set.

Plate 32's method

The Minkowski sausage, measured on this curve

log–log

Plate 32, on the next page of the book, wraps a curve in a band of radius ε: every point within ε of it. The band's area divided by its width 2ε is the curve's length as seen at resolution ε. For a smooth curve that settles to the true length. For a fractal it keeps growing, roughly like ε1−D, which is the law Richardson found for coastlines. Each line is one depth: band area ÷ 2ε, the curve's length as seen at resolution ε. The lines agree at large ε and peel away at small ε once the band is narrower than that depth's finest detail. After that point a depth's band stops changing, which is Plate 32's point. The fitted D is 1 − slope of the current depth's line for ε up to 0.03; it creeps toward 1.8687 only slowly as depth grows (a level-6 check gives about 1.80).
