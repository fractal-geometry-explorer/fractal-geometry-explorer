Plate 63

The original Peano curve, a square sweep

A quadric Koch construction of dimension D = 2 · N = 9, r = 1/3

The initiator is the unit square, the black box. On each side goes Peano's generator: nine steps of a third, with one square bump out and one in. The bumps touch, so the islands are black squares on a chunk of an infinite chessboard, and the coastline is a grid of lines a distance η = 3−n apart. The plate cuts off the corners, keeping the area the same, so you can see which squares join and which are separated by a channel.

No limit island

One would like a limit island, as in Chapter 6, but here there isn't one. A point chosen at random almost surely flips between land and sea forever as the stages go on. Meanwhile any square of middling size x, with η ≪ x ≪ 1, is cut into dry land and water in nearly equal parts.

Plate 63, large figure

One fourth of the coastline

Peano's curve on one side: 9n steps filling the square whose diagonal is that side

At stage 4 the whole island would merge into 50% gray, so the plate draws one of its four sides alone and larger. With rounded corners it "can be followed unambiguously (at some risk of becoming seasick)". It shows what is meant by saying the limit curve fills the plane. Each side's curve fills the square with that side as its diagonal, area ½.

Stagestage 3

With sharp corners the curve touches itself at the points it visits twice. Rounded, it neither crosses nor touches itself.

Without knowing the cascade that built it, Mandelbrot writes, one would be baffled by the long-range order that keeps these curves from crossing, or even touching, themselves. Any lapse in discipline would make self-contact very likely, and a total breakdown of discipline gives Brownian motion (Chapter 25), which crosses itself endlessly.

Plates 64 and 65

Cesàro's and Pólya's triangle sweeps, and variants

Two equal intervals at θ = 90° · N = 2, r = 1/√2 · D = 2

The simplest generator one could imagine: two equal intervals meeting at an angle θ between 90° and 180°. At θ = 180° it is a straight line; at 120° it gives the triadic Koch curve. At θ = 90°, D = 2 and the curve sweeps a triangle. Which triangle, and how, depends only on where each copy of the generator goes, to the right or the left of the previous teragon.

Plate 65, caption

The Kakutani–Gomory problem

Visit any points in the square in the order Cesàro's curve reaches them, and the squared steps add up to at most 4

The question. Scatter M points anywhere in a unit square and join them into one path. Score the path by squaring each step's length and adding these up. Is there always an order that scores under some fixed number, however many points there are? A random order doesn't: each step scores about 1/3 on average, so the total grows like M/3. Kakutani proved that some order always scores at most 8, and Gomory brought this down to 4.

Gomory's order. Run four Cesàro curves inward from the square's sides, one minute each, so that together they sweep the whole square in 4 minutes. Visit the points in the order the curve first reaches them. Two facts finish the proof:

  1. If the curve reaches Q t minutes after P, then P and Q are at most √t apart, so the step from P to Q scores at most t.
  2. The minutes between consecutive points add up to no more than the curve's 4 minutes, so the whole path scores at most 4.

Why Cesàro's curve. Fact 1 needs a curve that fills area slowly and never jumps, so that points it reaches close together in time are close together in space. Cesàro's curve is the slowest of the curves on this page: it never crosses itself, but its stages trace every grid line twice and pass each grid point four times, so an interval of length 1 fills only a quarter of a unit of area (table below). Mandelbrot calls it the slowest such curve on a square grid, without giving a proof. That slowness, a weakness for filling the plane, is exactly what fact 1 needs. With other Peano curves fact 1 can fail.
