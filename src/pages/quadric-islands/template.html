Plates 49–55Quadric Koch Islands

Koch islands grown from polygons, mostly squares. They start with nested islands and lakes whose coastlines run from almost smooth (D near 1) to D ≈ 1.37. Then come four square islands whose generators wander over a lattice, pushing D from 3/2 toward 2. Every generator here was read off the book's diagrams, and every figure is computed from it.

Plate 49

Alternative Koch islands and lakes

Coastline dimensions from 1 to D = log 3 / log √5 ≈ 1.3652

The initiator is a regular polygon with M sides. The generator has three equal legs, turning by θ = 360°/M and then back by θ, so the first and last legs are parallel. Plates 46–47 were the case M = 6. This plate nests advanced stages for M = 32, 16, 8 and 4, alternately as islands and lakes. The central island is hatched by Mandelbrot's quartet, a Peano curve that fills it (Plates 72–73).

As M grows, θ shrinks, each generator flattens toward a straight line and D falls toward 1. Pushed to M = ∞ the pattern would converge to a circle. Going inward, the shapes "shrivel", first gradually, then by rapid jumps. The next step, M = 3, gives D = 2 and a curve that runs into itself (Plates 72–73).

One rule, any polygon

Pick the number of sides. Every island keeps exactly the area of its polygon, since the generator is symmetric about its center point. Mandelbrot also lets each stage use the generator S or its mirror image F. With S and F alternating, the corners where a chord used to wind forever (Gosper's loxodromic points) become hyperbolic, like Koch's: the chord only swings back and forth.

A critical dimension

With [0, 1] as initiator, the angle at each bend of the generator can be anything from 180°, a straight line with D = 1, down to 60°, where D = 2. Below a critical angle the curve runs into itself. Mandelbrot puts that angle close to 60°. Computing the stages up to 10, we find the first real crossings at about 64.4°, where D ≈ 1.85. Every polygon from M = 4 up (90° and wider) is safely self-avoiding.

Plate 51

A quadric Koch island

Coastline dimension D = 3/2 = 1.5000

Plates 49 to 55 mostly start from a square, hence quadric. Here each side becomes 8 sides a quarter as long. As in Plates 46 to 49, the island's area never changes. Neither the stages nor the limit curve ever overlap, cross or even touch themselves, and the same is true through Plate 55. The fractal is the coastline; the land and the sea have ordinary, finite areas.

N = 8, r = 1/b = 1/4
D = log 8 / log 4 = 3/2

Plate 53

A quadric Koch island

Coastline dimension D = log 18 / log 6 ≈ 1.6131

Again a square, now with an 18-step generator. These islands share roughly the same overall outline only because the initiator is a square; their texture depends strongly on D. The generators also share a property Mandelbrot calls maximality.

N = 18, r = 1/b = 1/6
D = log 18 / log 6 ≈ 1.6131
Stagestage 3
Maximal generatorsb = 6: N = 18, D ≈ 1.6131

Take generators drawn on a square lattice that give self-avoiding curves with any lattice initiator. With steps of 1/b, the most steps they can have is N = b²/2 for even b and (b² + 1)/2 for odd b. All four generators in Plates 51–55 reach that maximum. Pushing D up this way gets more and more contrived; the limit D = 2 comes in the next chapter, with Peano curves.

A Richardson cascade?

Mandelbrot sees an uncanny resemblance between this sequence and turbulent dispersion of ink in water, as in a sketch by Corrsin (1959). A pinch of energy spreads a square blob of ink, the first eddy splits into smaller eddies, and the energy cascades to ever smaller scales until it adds only a slight fuzz to the blob's outline. That a Richardson cascade leads to a fractal boundary seems inescapable to him, but he calls the link to D = 5/3 shaky, since numerological analogies are not to be trusted. A postscript adds that Paul Dimotakis later photographed thin sections of a turbulent jet that look strikingly like Plate 55.
