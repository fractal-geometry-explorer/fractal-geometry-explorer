Plate 155Fractal Umbrella Trees

Grow a tree from two branches: at every fork, two new branches open at the same angle θ, shorter than their parent by the same ratio r. The branch tips form a dust of dimension D between 1 and 2, and for each D the plate takes θ as small as the tree can bear without touching itself. These trees are the book's first nonuniform fractals: tips and branches have dimensions that clash, and neither part can be ignored. Every figure is computed from the rules; nothing is traced.

Fractal umbrella trees and fractal canopies

N = 2 · tips D = log 2 / log(1/r) · θ the smallest angle compatible with self-avoidance

Five trees, one recipe. Each starts as a bare V — the plate draws no trunk — and every branch carries two children at the inter-branch angle θ, reduced by the ratio r. Reading across the plate, θ is 20°, 36° and 60° along the top, then 150° at the bottom left and 200° at the bottom right. For D barely above 1 the result is whisk-like, then broom-like; as D grows the branches open up, and past θ = 180° they curl backward into a pattern the caption likens to classical dance sculptures of India. The two bottom trees are printed lying on their side.

The five angles are exact: fitting the construction to a 600 dpi scan lands on 20°, 36°, 60°, 150° and 200° to a tenth of a degree, with r in every case at the self-contact threshold computed on this page, as the caption prescribes. Apex positions, sizes and the printed recursion depths (9 to 11 levels) are fitted to the scan. The three upright trees match to under 0.3 px at 600 dpi; the 200° tree matches everywhere, with about 1.5 px of drift on its longest lines, which looks like plotter or print distortion.

The construction

Grow a tree at the self-contact threshold

two branches per fork · lengths × r each level · D = log 2 / log(1/r)

The interesting trees are the barely self-avoiding ones. Keep θ fixed and lengthen the branches: at some ratio r the branch tips just reach each other, and past it the tree overlaps itself. This page finds that threshold live, from the rule that a tree first touches itself where an extremal branch tip reaches the line of symmetry between two sibling branches. Two landmarks fall out exactly: at θ = 120° the threshold is r = 1/φ ≈ 0.618, the golden ratio, and at θ = 180° it is r = 1/√2, where D = 2 and the tree fills a patch of the plane.

Past θ = 180° the branches bend backward, touch sooner, and the threshold D falls again — which is why the plate's 200° tree has a smaller D than its 150° one. The caption notes that D = 2 with θ = 180° fills the plane; this page's computed curve passes through that point. With the threshold released you can set r below 1/2: the branches then have finite total length, the tree's own dimension is 1 whatever its tips do, and Mandelbrot files such trees under the name subfractal.

Nonuniform fractals

Tips against branches

branch tips: DT = 0, D = log 2 / log(1/r) · each branch: DT = D = 1 · whole tree: D of the larger part

A tree is the sum of two parts whose dimensions clash. The branches dominate it intuitively and topologically: they form connected curves, and erasing one, two or three points disconnects a branch tip, a branch interior, or a fork. The tips, left on their own, are a dust like Cantor's, with N = 2 pieces of ratio r. Yet when D > 1 the whole tree's Hausdorff dimension is D, the tips' value — measured by dimension, the branches are negligible! A fractal whose parts disagree this way is what the chapter calls nonuniform: around a branch point the tree looks like a few intervals, around a tip it looks like a dust, so no single magnifying glass describes it everywhere.

At the threshold the tips touch each other asymptotically, so they stop being a dust (DT = 0) and merge into a connected curve (DT = 1) with the same fractal dimension — Mandelbrot names it the extended fractal canopy, and the curve bounding the open region outside the tree the canopy. Pick "tips only" and the canopy appears, folds and all; its vertical shadow lengthens as D grows. The chapter closes by remarking that branches ending inside the folds would wither for lack of sunshine — cauliflower and broccoli differ, it suggests, partly by a fractal dimension. The tips here are drawn at 13 levels, 8,192 dots.
