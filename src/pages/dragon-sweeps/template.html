Plates 66–67Square and Dragon Sweeps

The generator is the same one as in Plates 64 and 65: two intervals meeting at a right angle. What changes is the rule for which side each copy goes on, and seemingly slight changes there have lasting consequences: a square swept with no waste, a right trapezoid, and the dragon. Plate 66

A later square sweep by Peano

Initiator [0, 1] · two equal legs at 90° · N = 2, r = 1/√2 · D = 2 · area 1

Start from the unit interval with the square above it. Each step replaces every interval by the right-angled generator, as in Plate 64. What is new is how the side is chosen. Two stages on, every interval is again the side of its own small square, and these squares tile the big one exactly. The book shows stages 2, 4 and 6:

Efficiency, an extremal property

On an interval of length 1, this curve fills an area of 1. Pólya's and Cesàro's sweeps (Plates 64–65) and the dragon below fill only ½ or ¼. Mandelbrot notes that when the teragons lie on a square grid, the area swept can never be more than 1. It reaches that maximum when the curve avoids itself, as this one does once each strand is pulled into its own square. So avoiding self-contact is more than a matter of looks. Rounding off a curve's self-contacts, as in Plate 63, does not make it as efficient as a curve that truly avoids itself.

The squares show where the 1 comes from. At an even stage the 4k intervals have length 2−k, and each owns a square of that side. The squares never overlap, so their areas add up to exactly 1. Edges traced twice do no harm here, because the two passes own squares on opposite sides.

Hilbert's curve inside. Mandelbrot remarks that this sweep, with its self-contacts smoothed away, "falls back on a Peano curve due to Hilbert". The cleanest way to see it is to tick the last box above. At every even stage the curve visits its squares in exactly the order of Hilbert's curve (checked here up to stage 12, all 4,096 squares). Mandelbrot describes it as joining the midpoints of the intervals at alternate stages. Either way, the limit curve is Hilbert's.

Plate 67

A curve sweeping a right trapezoid

Two unequal legs at 90° · r₁ = cos 30°, r₂ = sin 30° · D = 2

Same rule as the square sweep, but the generator's two legs are unequal: the corner sits at angle φ from the interval instead of 45°. One more choice appears: which leg comes first. Putting the short leg first on the squares' sides, and the long leg first on their halves, turns the squares into right trapezoids, with parallel sides tan φ and cot φ on a base of length 1, and they still tile exactly. The caption says the processing is the same as in the preceding plate; the printed figure uses the thin chevrons of Plate 64, each pointing toward the corner the next stage would add.

The plate stops short of the trapezoid's sharp corner because the curve approaches it slowly: the longest intervals shrink by only cos φ per stage.

Plate 66

The Harter–Heighway dragon, and alternate dragons

Initiator [1, 0] · two equal legs at 90° · first copy always to the right · D = 2

(Gardner 1967; Davis & Knuth 1970.) The generator alternates between the right and the left of the teragon, exactly as in Pólya's triangle sweep. The only difference is that here the first copy goes to the right at every stage. The consequences are dramatic. At a mature stage the curve itself is lost and only its boundary shows, called the dragon curve; the sweep deserves to be called the dragon sweep.

Like any Koch curve, the dragon is self-similar: "Copies" colours the 2k smaller dragons it is made of. It is also cut into portions that join at narrow wasp waists. Zoom in on a pinch to see them. These portions are similar to one another, but not to the dragon itself. (The book prints its mature stage turned half a turn from the early stages in its caption box; this page keeps one orientation throughout.)

Plates 66–67

The twindragon

Initiator [0, 1] followed by [1, 0] · area 1 · skin D ≈ 1.5236

The 1977 Fractals points out that with the dragon's rules a more natural initiator is [0, 1] followed by [1, 0]: two dragons back to back. Together they sweep a twindragon. The same shape turns up in number representations (Knuth 1980). In base −1 + i, using only the digits 0 and 1, the numbers 0.d₁d₂d₃… fill exactly one twindragon.
