Plate 115Length–Area Relations

For circles, length = 2√π·√area; for squares, length = 4·√area. Within any family of similar standard shapes, length divided by √area is one number, fixed by the shape. Fractal coastlines break the rule — their length is infinite — but Mandelbrot repairs it: measure the coast with a yardstick G, and (G-length)1/D stands in for the length. The repaired ratio explains why rivers seem to defy geometry, and it fits six decades of clouds and rain areas on one straight line.

The fractal length–area relation

(G-length) ∝ (G-area)D/2 at a fixed yardstick G · Koch island D = log 4/log 3 ≈ 1.2619

Take geometrically similar islands with coastlines of dimension D > 1. Their true lengths are infinite, so length/√area is useless. Instead walk each coast with dividers set to one and the same yardstick G, planting a vertex every G: the number of steps times G is the G-length. Mandelbrot's claim: at fixed G the ratio (G-length)1/D/(G-area)1/2 takes the same value for every island in the family — so on a log–log chart of G-length against area, similar islands line up with slope D/2. For standard shapes D = 1 and the slope is the familiar 1/2.

The islands' linear sizes are 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, so their areas span a factor of 256. Each coast is a teragon carried far enough that its edges are much shorter than G, and the red polygons are genuine divider walks on it. Change G: the G-lengths all change, but the slope — and so D — stays. The proof in the book is three lines: with the island-sized yardstick G* = (G-area)1/2/1000 the polygons are similar, and switching from G* to G multiplies each length by (G/G*)1−D. The fitted D wobbles a little as G moves — a real effect of divider walks on Koch curves, whose measured length oscillates log-periodically with the yardstick — so it hovers around the true value rather than converging to it.

How winding is the Missouri river?

Hack (1957) measured rivers and found (basin area)0.5 ∝ (river length)1/1.2 — early writers read the odd exponent as proof that big basins are long and thin while small ones are chubby. The length–area relation explains it with self-similar basins instead: if the river is a fractal curve of dimension D from source to mouth, then (river's G-length)1/D and (basin's G-area)1/2 are both proportional to the straight distance from source to mouth. Hack's exponent gives D = 1.2: the local wiggles of the banks and the enormous global bends are equally irregular.

For rivers longer than about 100 km, J. E. Mueller finds the exponent drifts back to standard, D = 1 — maps of extremely long rivers are more nearly straight, suggesting the self-similarity has an outer cutoff of the order of 100 km. The cumulative length of all the rivers in a basin should by the same argument be proportional to the basin's area.

Log (perimeter) versus log (area) for clouds and rain areas

Perimeter ∝ areaD/2 with D = 4/3, fitted over at least six orders of magnitude · from Lovejoy 1982

The book's hunch that fractals model clouds was confirmed by Lovejoy's area–perimeter graph. The data combine radar observations of tropical Atlantic rain areas (rain rate above 0.2 mm/hr, filled dots) with geostationary-satellite infrared observations of cloud areas over the Indian Ocean (cloud-top temperature below −10 °C, open circles). The areas run from 1 km² to over 1,000,000 km² — "very few graphs in meteorology involve all the available data over an enormous range of sizes, and are nearly as straight as this one." The perimeter is measured at a fixed pixel yardstick, so by the length–area relation the slope is D/2.

The area–volume relation

(G-area)1/D ∝ (G-volume)1/3 · mammalian pia: A ∝ VD/3 with D ≈ 2.73 to 2.79

The same derivation works in space: a domain bounded by a fractal surface of dimension D obeys (G-area)1/D ∝ (G-volume)1/3. Mandelbrot's favourite case is the mammalian brain. Gray matter is roughly a layer of fixed thickness ε spread along the brain's surface membrane, the pia; and across species the ratio of white to gray matter is roughly constant. Then the pia's area must grow like the brain's whole volume, A ∝ V — which the area–volume relation reads as D = 3, a surface that comes within ε of filling space. A mouse's cortex can stay smooth; a whale's must fold, and the folding is purely geometric — no threat from Dolphin or Whale. The measured exponent is a little lower: A ∝ VD/3 with D/3 ≈ 0.91–0.93 (Jerison), so the pia is only partly space-filling, D between 2.73 and 2.79.

Micro-droplets, lungs and living membranes

Condensation gave the relation its first physical case: Frenkel, Band and Bijl pictured a vapour as isolated molecules with occasional bound clusters, and M. E. Fisher proposed that a condensing droplet's area and volume are related by area1/D ∝ volume1/3. Fisher evaluated D analytically with no concern for geometry; the unavoidable conjecture is that droplet surfaces are fractals of dimension D.

Living membranes supplied more. Light microscopy put the human lung's alveolar area near 80 m², electron microscopy near 140 m² — a discrepancy that dissolves once the membrane is fractal, D ≈ 2.17 over a wide range of scales (Weibel). In liver cells, Paumgartner and Weibel reconciled conflicting area-per-volume estimates with D ≈ 2.09 for the outer mitochondrial membrane, 2.53 for the inner ones, and 1.72 for the endoplasmic reticulum.

Modular computer geometry. Rent's rule, credited within IBM to E. Rent: a module with C components talks to its surroundings through about T ∝ CD/E terminals. Keyes's reading is exactly the area–volume relation — components fill the module's volume, connections pass through its surface, so C1/E and T1/D both measure the module's radius. Early circuits gave D/E = 2/3, the standard surface-to-volume exponent. A shift register has T = 2 however big C is: D = 0. Full parallelism needs T = C: D = E, a Peano boundary. Everything between is a boundary of fractal dimension.
