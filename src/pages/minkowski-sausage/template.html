An example of Minkowski sausage

N = 8, r = 1/4 · D = log 8 / log 4 = 3/2 · band area ÷ 2ε ∝ ε1−D

Pick a radius ε and draw a disc of that radius around every point of the curve. The union of the discs is the band, Minkowski's sausage. Its area divided by its width 2ε is the curve's length as seen at resolution ε. For a smooth curve that number settles to the true length; for this one it keeps growing like ε1−D = ε−1/2. The caption says the curve is built "later (Plate 49)": in this edition that is the run of square-grid Koch plates, 49 to 55, and the curve is Plate 51's — each step replaced by eight steps a quarter as long, doubling the length at every stage.

Mandelbrot's point on page 32: the band changes while the construction adds detail larger than ε, and stops changing once the new detail is smaller. A stage's new bumps are its step size, 4−n of the base. Turn on the highlight and step through the stages — the changed area shrinks to slivers once 4−n falls below ε. Page 32 draws the fourth stage, 4,096 steps, traced from right to left, with the first third of the third stage below it; both match the generator step for step.

Each line is one stage: band area ÷ 2ε, the curve's length as seen at resolution ε. The lines share one path at large ε and leave it, levelling off, once ε drops below that stage's step. Along the shared path the slope is 1 − D, which is the law Richardson found for coastlines on this same page of the book. The fitted D uses the current stage for ε up to 0.03.

The local slope does not sit still at −1/2. It swings a little each time ε shrinks by a factor of four, because the curve's structure repeats at exactly that ratio. Fitting across a full swing averages it out: stage 6 gives about 1.49.
