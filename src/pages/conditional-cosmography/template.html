Chapter 22Conditional Cosmography

Chapter 21 gave the usual reasons for randomness; this chapter gives the fractal one — a nonrandom fractal cannot be stationary and cannot scale at every ratio, so no nonrandom model can satisfy any cosmographic principle. The repair is to weaken the principle itself: ask invariance not for all observers, but only for observers standing on matter. Chapter 22, pages 206–208

Peeking from a material point

conditional: M(R) ∝ Rᴰ from every material origin · absolute: a random ball is almost surely empty

The cosmologic principle — no place is special — read descriptively and brutally demands strict translation invariance; soften it by chance and it becomes statistical stationarity, the uniform cosmographic principle. But a fractal distribution of matter with D < 3 has global density zero, and a frame of reference dropped at random then almost surely sees nothing at all: the absolute distribution says only "empty", and distinguishes no D from another. Condition the frame's origin to lie on a material point — where every astronomer in fact stands — and the description comes alive: the conditional law is the same for all such observers, and M(R) ∝ Rᴰ.

This page's own illustration: the matter is a random curdling dust (base 3, every cell keeps N of its 9 ninths — chapter 23's construction, chosen here because it satisfies the conditional principle by design). The plane stands in for space, so the global-density exponent 3 becomes 2 and "δ = 0" reads "a vanishing share of the window's area". The M(R) chart averages fifty origins of each kind; Bayes connects the two laws — the absolute is the conditional averaged over uniformly scattered origins, which is exactly why it washes out to "empty" when δ = 0. The chapter's deadlock is drawn too: hierarchical models with a finite upper cutoff (Fournier universes scattered uniformly) rescue a positive δ at the price of making clusters local — the choice Mandelbrot's conditional principle avoids.

Chapter 22, pages 208–209

Stratified against scaling

nonrandom cascades scale only at ratios rᵏ · random dusts scale with respect to every r

A nonrandom fractal whose parts shrink by one ratio r admits only the scaling ratios rᵏ: it is strongly stratified, a built-in hierarchy of levels. Astronomers found such models computable but suspect — there is no direct evidence that the clusters are real — and called for quasi-continuous alternatives (de Vaucouleurs 1970). No nonrandom fractal can oblige. A random dust can: it scales with respect to every ratio, carries no designed-in strata, and still looks hierarchical, because apparent clustering is a corollary of any D < 2 — the hierarchy is in the eye, not the rule.

The stratified example is chapter 9's Fournier quincunx at ratio 1/5 (D = log 5/log 5 = 1 in the plane); its mass curve from the centre is a staircase — plateaus a factor 5 wide in R, risers a factor 5 in M, chapter 9's climb-and-sag band. The random partner is the curdling dust with N = 3, whose D = log 3/log 3 = 1 matches, and whose mass curve from a material point is straight. The point is the shape, not the slope: rescale the staircase by anything but a power of 5 and it misses itself, while the random curve slides along itself at any ratio, statistically.

The program the random chapters must fill

The chapter ends on a promissory note, redeemed in chapters 32 to 35: explicit random constructions that induce a zero global density; satisfy the conditional cosmographic principle and no stronger form; scale with respect to every ratio; are not stratified by design but look hierarchical as a corollary of D < 2; and fit the quantitative data better as the models grow more careful — the Lévy dusts, the trema dusts and their generalizations.
