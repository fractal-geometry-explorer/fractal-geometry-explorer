Plates 306–309Disc and Sphere Tremas

Punch round holes — tremas — out of the plane at random: every center is equally likely, and the p-th largest hole has area K(2−D)/p. Craters on the Moon really are spread this way, which is why these plates look like a bombarded surface. What the holes fail to cover is a fractal of dimension D and of zero area: a wildly extrapolated Swiss cheese. The same device with balls in space was Mandelbrot's second model of the galaxy clusters. Every figure is computed from the caption's rule; change the seed for another sample from the same law.

Plates 306 and 307

Smallish round tremas, in white: slices of "Swiss cheese"

trema of rank p: a disc of area K(2−D)/p · the black remainder has dimension D = 1.9900, then 1.9000

The tremas are white discs with centers thrown uniformly on the plane; ranked by size, disc p has area K(2−D)/p, so there is one large hole for every thousand pinholes. With D = 1.9900 the holes are small and rare, and the black slice "resembles a cheese that is almost entirely pierced by very small pin holes" — a wild extrapolation of Appenzeller. At D = 1.9000 the holes grow and overlap into an equally wild Emmenthaler. The extrapolation is wild because it never stops: ever smaller tremas keep falling forever, so the black "cheese" has zero area, and what you see at any resolution is only the part not yet eaten below that resolution.

Plates 308 and 309

Larger round tremas, in black: random forked white threads

the same construction with bigger tremas · the white remainder has dimension D = 1.7500, then 1.5000

The rule is unchanged; only the constant K = C/2 is bigger, because C = 2−D has grown. Now "hardly anything is left out", so the book switches ink: the tremas print in black and the surviving fractal in white. At D = 1.75 the survivors still read as a speckled ground between craters. By D = 1.5 they have thinned to the caption's "random forked white threads". Mandelbrot conjectures what happens next: as long as C stays small the remainder is a σ-cluster of connected filaments, of topological dimension 1; when D falls through a critical dimension Dcrit the filaments collapse, and the remainder is a dust.

Chapter 33, pages 301–305

Saturating the Moon, and galaxies from ball tremas

craters: Nr(area > s) = C/s per km² · the uncovered set has D = 2 − C, empty once C ≥ 2 · in space D = 3 − C

There is solid evidence that lunar craters follow exactly this law: the number with area above s km², centered in a given km², is C/s. If the Moon scooped up meteorites forever, every point would be covered again and again; the set left uncovered is nontrivial only because C — which measures the time since lava last wiped the surface clean, or the length of the bombardment — is finite. Following page 303, this figure keeps the seed invariant and lets C grow: each crater keeps its place and dilates — here the craters in black, the virgin surface left white — and the surface decays toward dust, with D = 2 − C falling as C climbs to 2.

The spatial version replaces discs by balls, with Nr(volume > v) = C/v and D = 3 − C. When D is close to the observed 1.23, the remainder is a dust whose points cluster like Lévy-flight stopovers — but no longer threaded along a trail, and its three- and four-point covariances fit the galaxy maps better than the flight of Chapter 32. Mandelbrot's simulations put Dcrit above 1.23, which the model needs: veils (DT = 2) split into filaments past one critical D, and filaments into dust past a second.

By D ≈ 1.2 the virgin set's grains are thinner than a pixel and vanish into the ink; the red overlay marks exactly the pixels whose center no drawn crater covers. The marked share swings wildly from seed to seed — from nothing to over 1% here — because a handful of window-sized tremas decide the window's fate; the readout's figure is the average over all seeds, and only that average follows the formula. The slider is logarithmic in C, matching how the plates are spaced (C = 0.01, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5). The chapter's appendix covers nonscaling craters, Nr ∝ 1/sγ with γ ≠ 1: for γ > 1 small craters dominate every point of the surface, the texture of Callisto; for γ < 1 with bounded crater sizes a positive area survives. Its closing aside computes that meteorites in space, which follow the same 1/v law with a constant of 10⁻²⁰, would as overlapping tremas leave a set of codimension 3 − D = 10⁻²⁰ — a limit "attained so extraordinarily slowly" that it means nothing in practice.
