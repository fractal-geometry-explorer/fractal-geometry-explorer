Plates 293–300Spatial Lévy Dusts

Mandelbrot's early model of galaxy clusters: a rocket jumps in random directions, and when the jump lengths follow the hyperbolic law Pr(U>u) = u−D, its stopovers form a dust of any dimension D below 2 — clustered into seeming hierarchies although none was put in. The chapter builds these dusts by subordination: run Brownian motion, but keep only the moments in a fractal subset of time. Every figure here is computed from the rules in the captions; the random ones are seeded, matched to the plates statistically rather than point for point.

a faulty run of the program behind Plate 69 · the strict order designed into that plate is hidden here

Plate 293 is the output of the program that was meant to draw the snowflake sweep of Plate 69, with a bug in it. Mandelbrot kept the recorded output after the bug was found and fixed: "the change that had been wrought by a single tiny bug in a critical place had gone well beyond anything we had expected", and the result "could pass for High Art". The book never says what the bug was, so the plate cannot be reproduced. Instead, this figure runs the same program — the 13-interval snowflake sweep, filled on one side — and lets you plant a bug of your own in a critical place: pick legs of the generator and corrupt how their copies are placed.

Plate 295

Nonrandom subordination: a clustered dust with D = 1

N = 16, r = 1/16 · D = 1, subordinate to a Koch curve of D = 3/2 · subordinator D = log 16/log 64 = 2/3 · (2/3) × (3/2) = 1

Subordination, before any randomness: take the Koch generator of Plate 49 — eight legs of length 1/4, dimension log 8/log 4 = 3/2 — and imagine a rubber band stretched along it. Pin the nine corners, cut each leg in the middle, and let the 16 pieces snap back to their unstretched length 1/16. Repeat inside every piece. The limit is a dust with N = 16 and r = 1/16, so D = 1: the dimension of a line, in a set that is nothing like one. The only points kept are the positions of the Koch motion at a fractal subset of time of dimension log 16/log 64 = 2/3, and (2/3) × (3/2) = 1 is the rule of multiplication of dimensions.

Plates 296 and 297, caption on page 294

Lévy flight and its stopovers: model clusters with D = 1.2600

jumps isotropic in space, lengths Pr(U>u) = u−D for u ≥ 1 · D = 1.26, near the D ≈ 1.23 of actual galaxies

A Lévy flight in 3-space: from each stopover the rocket jumps in a random, isotropic direction, and the jump length obeys the hyperbolic law above. Two independent flights leave the origin, standing for one flight watched forward and backward in time, so every stopover has the same claim to be the Center of the World — the conditional cosmographic principle. The top figure draws the trail with its "contrails"; the bottom figure wipes the jumps away, takes a photographic negative, and each remaining cell of a fine grid is a star or a galaxy. The plates are the projections of one flight on two perpendicular planes: the book suggests holding it half open to see the cluster in space.

Plate 298

Decrease of D by subordination

a planar Brown trail, D = 2, decimated again and again · subordinator D = 2−1/6 ≈ 0.89 · D = 1.78, 1.59, 1.41, 1.26, 1.12, 1.00, 0.89

Start from a planar Brownian trail, whose dimension is 2. Keep only the moments of time in a linear Lévy dust of dimension α = 2−1/6 ≈ 0.89, and the trail's dimension drops to 2α = 1.78. Ride a second decimation on the first and it drops to 1.59; each further subordination multiplies D by 0.89, down to 0.89 itself. For D close to 2 a "ghost" of the continuous Brown trail still shows; as D falls, clustering sharpens — not because points concentrate, but because most points disappear, leaving ever more apparent hierarchic levels. The black staircase beside each dust is the Lévy staircase: how time was decimated to make this dust from its predecessor.

The D = 1.00 view is, by construction, a Cauchy flight's dust — the text's example of subordination, where the "humorless" sections of a Brownian trail in 4-space have dimension exactly 1. The plate prints seven panels from one experiment; here one canvas steps through the same seven dusts of one seeded trail, so each is genuinely a decimation of the one before.

Plate 299

Zooming toward a Lévy dust with D = 1.2600

each step divides the field of vision by b = 3 · the detail changes, the broad lines do not

The book's six windows are one planar Lévy cluster of 12,500,000 positions seen from a spaceship that closes in: between views, the distance and the field of vision divide by b = 3. Because the dust is statistically self-similar, the structure in the window changes in detail but stays the same in kind. Here the same experiment runs live: step from view to view, or pan and zoom freely; the square marks the next window.
