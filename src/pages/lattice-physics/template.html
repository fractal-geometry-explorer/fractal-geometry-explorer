Chapter 36Fractals in Lattice Physics

Statistical physics replaces its hardest problems by analogs on a lattice — and on a lattice, a fractal can never be built the way a Koch curve is built, by interpolating detail forever. The lattice shapes only reach their fractal limits "weakly": each downsizing is a fresh shape, a little closer to some limit, without ever settling on one. This chapter's single figure makes the one case where the limit can be exhibited: a flipped spin whose influence grows into the Sierpiński gasket. Around it the chapter walks through the self-avoiding walk of polymer physics and a random model of river networks.

A spin impurity snowballs into the gasket

S(t, n) = +1 when S(t−1, n−1) ≠ S(t−1, n+1) · the +'s are the odd binomial coefficients · downsized limit: the Sierpiński gasket, D = log 3/log 2 ≈ 1.5850

A line of spins, all −1, is left alone by the rule: each spin becomes −1 when its two neighbors agree, +1 when they differ. Flip one spin at t = 0 and follow the purely local rule. The disturbance snowballs — "local interactions snowball into global effects" — and the +'s mark the odd coefficients of Pascal's triangle. Join neighboring +'s and the graph is kin to the Sierpiński gasket of chapter 14; downsize it, and it converges to the gasket itself: the chapter's one "example of demonstrable weak limit."

Chapter 36, pages 326–328

Two kinds of convergence

Koch teragons settle on one curve (a strong limit) · downsized random walks never settle (a weak limit)

The bulk of the Essay builds fractals by recursive interpolation: each Koch teragon keeps everything the last stage drew and adds finer wiggles, "like a picture to which detail is continually added by sharper focusing." A downsized random walk cannot be refined that way — there is no way to interpolate a random walk. Each downsizing is a brand-new walk: first seeming close to some Brownian motion, "then even closer to a different one, then still closer to yet another one, and so on... without ever settling down." Mathematicians call the first limit strong, the second weak — and on a lattice, weak is all there is.

"Strays by" measures how far each curve moved from its own previous refinement (the largest distance from the new curve to the old one, both scaled to the same size). The Koch figure shrinks it by 1/3 per stage; the walk's stays large forever, because each refinement is an independent walk — the honest meaning of a weak limit. The walk here takes 4k lattice steps of length 2−k, the downsizing under which random walks converge weakly to Brownian motion — the one case, the chapter notes, with a full mathematical proof.

The self-avoiding walk and linear polymers

after n steps, displacement R ≈ n1/D · Flory: D = (E + 2)/3, so D = 4/3 in the plane · Brownian motion has D = 2

A linear polymer is a chain of molecules that cannot cross itself. Its lattice model is the self-avoiding random walk: forward at random, but never through a point twice and never into a trap. "So difficult that to date no analytical study has been successful" — yet simulations, due to C. Domb, found that after n steps the walk has wandered about n1/D from home, with D ≈ 4/3 in the plane: within a circle of radius R around a site sit about RD others, the signature of a fractal of dimension 4/3. Self-avoidance swells the chain — Brownian motion's D = 2 means n1/2; the polymer spreads much farther.

Chapter 36, pages 332–333

Rivers' failure to run straight

Hack: river length ∝ (drainage area)D/2 · straight rivers would give D = 1 · real maps give D between 1.2 and 1.3

Hack's empirical law says a river's length grows like the D/2 power of its drainage area, with D between 1.2 and 1.3 — rivers run crooked. Leopold & Langbein explained this with a lattice simulation that "ought to interest the physicist": sources are chosen at random, each stream wanders into adjacent squares until it leaves the region or joins an earlier stream, and the procedure continues until the whole grid drains. Often the second stream — "the Missouri" — turns out longer than the river it joins. Their simulations gave length ∝ area0.64, hence D ≈ 1.28: close to Domb's 4/3, and Mandelbrot suspects the gap is genuine, since every stream is hemmed in by its neighbors.
