The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 3, "Dimension, Symmetry, Divergence" · pages 14–19

Chapter 3What Is a Fractal?

The word the whole book rests on is defined in this chapter, and the definition is a comparison of two dimensions: a fractal is a set whose Hausdorff–Besicovitch dimension D strictly exceeds its topological dimension D_T. Alongside it the chapter sets a second, deliberately loose notion — the effective dimension an observer assigns at a given resolution — and previews the symmetries and divergences the rest of the book lives on. These six pages print no figures, so both figures here are this page's own illustrations of the text.

Pages 14–15

The definition: D against D_T

D = log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.2618 · D_T = 1 · D > D_T: a fractal

Mathematics offers many dimensions, and for Euclid's shapes they all agree. Two matter here. The topological dimension D_T of Brouwer, Lebesgue, Menger and Urysohn is always a whole number: 0 for a dust of disconnected points, 1 for a curve. The dimension D of Hausdorff and Besicovitch need not be whole, and the two satisfy only the Szpilrajn inequality D ≥ D_T. When they agree, the set is dimensionally concordant, and Euclid covers it. When they disagree there was no word at all, so Mandelbrot coined one: "A fractal is by definition a set for which the Hausdorff Besicovitch dimension strictly exceeds the topological dimension."

Pages 17–18

Effective dimension: a ball of thread

a 10 cm ball of 1 mm thread · effective dimension 0 → 3 → 1 → 3 → 1 → 0 as the resolution refines

Besides the two exact dimensions the chapter invokes a third, "a notion that should not be defined precisely": the dimension an object has in effect. Physicists already use it — a fine veil is treated as 2-dimensional, a thread as 1, a tiny ball as 0, each a model chosen because its corrections are smallest. The chapter's test case is a ball 10 cm across wound of thread 1 mm thick. Seen from far away it is a point. At 10 cm resolution it is a solid three-dimensional ball; at 10 mm, a mess of one-dimensional threads; at 0.1 mm each thread is a three-dimensional column; at 0.01 mm the column dissolves into one-dimensional fibers; and at atomic scales the ball is a finite crowd of pinpoints, dimension 0 again.

The closing move of the section is the bridge to everything that follows: in a fractal, an ill-defined transition between well-defined zones widens into a whole zone within which D > D_T. The same crossovers return in earnest for a sheet of paper, and for every natural fractal's outer and inner cutoffs.
