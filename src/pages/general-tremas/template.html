The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 35, "General tremas, and the control of texture" · pages 319–325

Plates 323 and 325General Tremas

A trema fractal is what is left of space after random holes — tremas — are cut out of it. A theorem of El Hélou says the dimension D cares only about how the tremas' areas or volumes are distributed, so their shape is a free knob: it tunes the texture of the remainder — its lacunarity and its succolarity — while D stands still. This was the last chapter of the book to be written, "a mere sketch" against a deadline, and its two plates show the knob at work in the plane and in space.

Plate 323

Effect of the trema generator upon lacunarity

Diamond tremas, centers and sizes Poisson · tremas with area above a: (2 − D)/2a per unit area · D fixed by the area law alone (El Hélou 1978)

Both panels cut diamonds out of a square: the left panel's diamonds are nearly squares, the right panel's are sharp needles, isotropically rotated. Both use the very same dimension D and the same areas for the largest and smallest trema, so the white remainders have identical expected areas — the theorem again. Yet one remainder huddles into clumps and the other spreads nearly everywhere: the needle panel has far lower lacunarity. The needles also slice the remainder into many small polygons, which is the chapter's other claim: flattening the tremas drives the critical percolation dimension Dcrit as close to 2 as one pleases.

A random plate, matched statistically, not stroke for stroke. The book states no numbers for these panels: the aspects (0.75 and 0.02), D = 1.5 and the area cutoffs (largest 1/20 of the square, smallest 3·10⁻⁵) were fitted to the printed ink coverage, and the isotropic rotation follows the chapter's text. In both panels the readout pairs run left panel · right panel. The white area is measured by sampling 20,000 random points against the diamonds themselves; Λ is the mean of M² over the squared mean of M, the white mass in boxes of side 1/8 of the square: identical areas, very different Λ.

Plate 325

Fractal dust from nonspherical tremas, on a spherical sky

A 600³ torus · ellipsoid tremas, volume pinned to scale³, scales 2.3 to 300 · tremas with volume above u: (3 − D)/3u per unit volume · plate: D ≈ 1.23, shells 400 < R ≤ 600

The caption opens with a confession: this is not the intended illustration, and its precise specification "escapes me at the moment" — the files of the 1979 runs were lost. What it does describe is the pipeline, reproduced here: cut ellipsoid tremas out of a 600³ lattice whose opposite faces are glued (a torus), move the origin to a point no trema removed, keep the lattice points of the shell 400 < R ≤ 600 sorted into shells of decreasing brightness, push each point off the lattice by a random [0,1] vector, and map the octant x, y, z > 0 — northern latitudes, longitudes −45° to 45° — through the Hammer projection. The surviving dust clusters on the sky like galaxy counts, which is chapter 9's seed for this whole family of models.

A reconstruction of the described pipeline, not of the lost picture: since the book never says what the nonspherical tremas were, this page uses ellipsoids with volume pinned to scale³ — flattened 10 : 3 by default, needle-like 1 : 0.4 or spheres for comparison — and the trema scales are truncated to 2.3 … 300, which fixes the predicted share (2.3/300)^(3 − D). The brightness steps, the octant window and the caption's warning that the lattice-smoothing jitter "misrepresents the underlying fractal" are all kept; turn the jitter off to see the bare lattice points. The antipodal octant re-uses the very same tremas, as the caption suggests.
