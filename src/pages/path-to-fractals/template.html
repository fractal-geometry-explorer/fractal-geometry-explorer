Thirty years, five fields, one thread

The involvement was triggered in 1951 by a casual side interest in Zipf's law of word frequencies, met through a book review retrieved from a wastebasket for subway reading. Accounting for it was easy, and done — but it left a sensitivity to scaling regularities everywhere: in incomes, in prices, in noises, in turbulence, in river records, in coastlines, in the clustering of galaxies. Each station added tools; none followed a plan. The theory grew bottom-up, and the overviews — 1964, 1971, 1973–74 — only named what the casework had already built.

Chapter 42, pages 422–423

Description before explanation

Why the long "disregard for explanation through models"? Because the early models kept betraying their predictions: minor changes in seemingly insignificant assumptions provoked drastic changes in what a model foretold. The standard central limit theorem "explains" every Gaussian — until one learns there are other central limit theorems with other outcomes, at which point the argument stops persuading: an explanation more complicated than its outcome, with equally plausible variants predicting the opposite, brings no understanding. A revival of Yule's 1922 taxonomy model even convinced readers that scaling in social science was universally explained and therefore (!) beneath attention. So the method became: accept scaling as seen, explore its geometric consequences with care, and let the explanations come when they are real — as they do in chapters 11 and 20, "and I rejoice in them".

The payoff came with prices: economic theory dared no prediction about the structure of price records, and the common statistics extracted nothing — Leontief's "massive and sophisticated machinery … with such indifferent results". Scaling worked amazingly well, because it incorporates exactly the two striking characteristics of competitive prices: high discontinuity, and cycles without periods.

Chapter 42, page 424

An historical anomaly

Given how geometric the early studies of turbulence and critical phenomena were, a theory of fractals "should" have grown inside physics. It did not. It grew in the branches of low competitiveness — linguistics' statistics, economics' records, hydrology's ranges — where a lone worker could stay for decades. New concepts rarely enter science that way any more; fractal geometry, the epilog ends, is such an anomaly.
