Pages 166–179Self-Inverse Fractals and Apollonian Nets

Nearly every fractal so far was left unchanged by similarities, which shrink and translate. This chapter asks instead for the smallest sets left unchanged by inversions in a set of generating circles — a question posed by Poincaré and Klein in the 1880s, whose answer's actual shape stayed unexplored until Mandelbrot's construction in this chapter. The simplest case is the Apollonian net, which also models the layering of a smectic liquid crystal — soap. Every figure here is computed from the book's rules; the generating circles are fitted from the scans.

Pages 167–168

Standard geometric inversion

Inversion in a circle of centre O and radius R: P ↦ P′ on the same ray from O, with |OP| · |OP′| = R²

After the line, the next simplest shape in Euclid is the circle, and being a circle survives inversion. Inversion in a circle C of centre O swaps inside and outside: a point at distance d from O lands at distance R²/d on the same ray. The chapter restates the facts its figures illustrate: circles through O invert into straight lines, and conversely; circles elsewhere invert into circles; circles orthogonal to C, and lines through O, are left in place.

A set that inversions carry onto itself is called self-inverse. The rest of the chapter looks for the smallest closed self-inverse set of a family of generating circles — its limit set L, the region "where infinitesimal children concentrate".

Pages 169–170

Apollonian packing, nets and gaskets

D is not known in closed form: 1.300197 < D < 1.314534 (Boyd 1973), numerically D ≈ 1.3058

Apollonius of Perga gave the algorithm for the circles tangent to three given circles. When the three are mutually tangent — two inside the third, as in the book's figures — exactly two Apollonian circles answer, the largest circles inscribed in the two curvilinear triangles. Five circles now bound six triangles; inscribing the largest circle in each, and repeating without end, is Apollonian packing. The circles plus their limit points form the Apollonian net; the portion inside one triangle is a gasket.

Apollonian nets are self-inverse

Four Γ circles → four generating circles C₁…C₄, each orthogonal to a triplet of Γ's · the net splits into 4 clans

Return to the three tangent circles, add either Apollonian circle, and call the four the Γ circles. Each triplet of Γ's has a circle orthogonal to all three — through their three tangency points — and these four new circles are taken as the generator, C₁, C₂, C₃, C₄. The payoff: the smallest closed set self-inverse with respect to the C circles is the Apollonian net built on the Γ circles. Every circle of the net is carried to one of the Γ circles by a unique sequence of inversions, so the net sorts into four clans.

Here the net is not computed by Descartes' theorem at all: every circle is an inverted image of a Γ circle under a reduced word in the four C inversions, and the colours name which Γ each descends from — the book's four clans, made visible. That the result coincides with the packing of the previous section is the self-inverse property. Like the Sierpiński gasket of Plate 141, the net can also be knitted with a single thread: a line can pass twice over certain points but never twice over any interval.

Poincaré chains, and Poincaré's construction of L

M = 4 circles, each tangent to its two neighbours · stage s shows all 4·3ˢ links · the chain thins to its thread L

A Poincaré chain is a necklace of M circles, each tangent to its two cyclic neighbours and meeting no other. Its limit set L is then a loop — a Jordan curve. To draw L, Poincaré and Fricke & Klein replace the chain, in stages, by chains of more and smaller links: the first stage replaces every link Cᵢ by the inverses in Cᵢ of the other links, creating M(M−1) = 12 smaller ones, and each later stage inverts the whole previous chain in each original circle. As in the book's figure, each stage is drawn in ink over the previous stage in white, on the gray panel.

A self-inverse fractal: Mandelbrot's osculating discs

Γijk ⟂ Cᵢ, Cⱼ, Cₖ · the tangency-free disc Δijk osculates L · clans of the Δ's tile everything but L

A standard curve is hugged, to second order, by its osculating circle. Curvature means nothing on L, but at every point where two packing circles touch they visibly "embrace" the rest of the set between them, and Mandelbrot extends this: a circle osculates L when it is critical between lying all on one side and not. For the same chain as above, the circles Γijk orthogonal to the triplets Cᵢ, Cⱼ, Cₖ osculate L, and each bounds a disc Δijk free of the chain's tangency points. The clans of the Δ's — their inverses and repeated inverses — cover the whole plane except L itself: gray outside, black inside. A few stages already outline L better than many stages of Poincaré's chains.

Self-homographic fractals, near the Peano limit

An homography z ↦ (az + b)/(cz + d), ad − bc = 1 · inversion ∘ reflection ∘ rotation · a reconstruction, not the plate

Groups of inversions matter to mathematicians through their relation with groups of homographies — Möbius maps, each a product of an inversion, a reflection and a rotation. Without rotation, homographies learn everything from inversions; allowing rotation "brings in new riches". Plate 178 is such a limit set, an almost plane-filling shape devised by David Mumford for this book. Its exact group is not stated in the book, so the plate cannot be reproduced stroke for stroke. This figure instead lets you add the rotation yourself: each generator is inversion in a chain circle followed by a rotation about that circle's centre, which makes every two-letter word an homography. At 0° the limit set is the thread of the sections above; as the twist grows it spirals and thickens toward the plane-filling "Peano limit".

A celebrated self-inverse fractal, corrected

Fricke & Klein's Figure 156 (1897): five generating circles · four form a Poincaré chain; C₅ touches C₂ and C₃

Fricke's Figure 156 claimed to show the self-inverse fractal of five generating circles, and was reproduced very widely. Fricke knew that L incorporates circles and had his draftsman include them — but otherwise he did not know what sort of very irregular shape to expect, and Mandelbrot's verdict on the discrepancy is "horrid". The osculating σ-disc construction gives the actual shape. The five circles here are fitted from the engraving as the book reproduces it; four of them form a Poincaré chain, whose own Jordan curve L* also belongs to the full set L, along with its transforms by the remaining inversions.
