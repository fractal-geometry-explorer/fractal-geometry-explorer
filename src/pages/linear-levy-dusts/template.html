Plates 285–287Linear Lévy Dusts

The Cantor dust of chapter 8 was a first model of noise bursts on a line, but it is far too regular: its origin is a privileged point, and its ratios are powers of 1/3. Chapter 31 randomizes it. Cut random intervals — tremas — out of the line, the number longer than u growing as 1/u, and what survives is Paul Lévy's dust: the one self-similar dust whose past and future are independent, seen from any of its points. The plates picture it sideways — streets cut at random through a city block, and devil's staircases piling the dust's mass. Every figure is computed from the rules in the captions; change the seed for another sample from the same law.

Chapter 31, pages 280–284

Interval tremas and the Lévy dust

tremas longer than u, per unit length: (1 − Dt)/u · survivors: a Lévy dust of D = max(Dt, 0)

Throw open intervals at the line, independently, centers everywhere equally likely; the p-th longest trema in view has length proportional to 1/p. Tremas overlap "with gusto", so the gaps they merge into are longer than the tremas themselves. Each row keeps only the tremas above a threshold ε, and ε falls by 3 from row to row: the surviving stretches shorten, split and sometimes vanish — bursts within bursts, with no grid and no privileged origin. When Dt > 0 the survivors have a positive chance of never dying out, and the limit dust has dimension D = Dt. When Dt ≤ 0 the line is almost surely covered whole.

The trema law is the text's: the number of tremas centered in a stretch Δt with length above u has expectation (1 − Dt)Δt/u and a Poisson distribution; here the p-th longest has length exactly κ/p with the stated expectation, and only the centers are random. Tremas centered outside the window still reach into it, so the window is cut from a longer sampled line. The side-by-side view puts a Cantor dust and a Lévy dust of the same dimension together: equal D, but the Lévy dust's gaps fall where chance puts them.

Plate 285

Random pattern of streets

straight strips, isotropic, widths with Nr(W > w) ∝ 1/w · blocks of houses: D < 2 · any line cuts them in a Lévy dust of D − 1

A dust is "thin and spare to the point of being invisible", so the plate shows one indirectly, the way the triadic Koch curve makes the Cantor dust visible on its base line. Black street-like strips fall across the plane at random — directions isotropic, widths hyperbolic, soon too thin to draw. The white remainder, the "blocks of houses", has zero area and dimension D below 2. As long as D > 1, an arbitrary straight line meets the blocks in a linear Lévy dust of dimension D − 1; for D < 1 the cut is almost surely empty.

Plates 286 and 287

Paul Lévy's devilish staircases

graph dimension 1 · the risers' abscissas form Lévy dusts of D = 9/10, 3/10 and 0.6309

These are randomized cousins of Plate 83's devil's staircase: the largest one has the Cantor original's dimension, and the two small ones sit far above and far below it. The recipe is the caption's. Climb the ordinate in equal steps Δy; at each step the abscissa jumps ahead by a random amount U with Pr(U > u) = u−D; then rescale so the staircase ends at (1, 1). The treads are the gaps of a Lévy dust, and the risers' abscissas are the dust itself — the staircase is the dust's mass M(R), just as the Cantor function is the Cantor bar's. For D = .3 the jumps are so wildly unequal that a few giants swallow nearly the whole width.

Random plates, matched statistically with the caption's own parameters: Δy = .002 as printed, so 500 risers, with the seed exposed. Like the Cantor staircase, each one climbs its whole height on a set of measure zero — treads sum to the full width, risers to the full height, so the graph's length is exactly 2 and its dimension 1, however wild the treads. The staircase with D = 1/2 is a familiar object in disguise: the zeroset of Brownian motion (chapter 25) is precisely the Lévy dust with D = 1/2. Chapter 29 returns here from the other side: fill every dip of a Brown line-to-line function to its brim, and the record of the fill levels is such a staircase.
