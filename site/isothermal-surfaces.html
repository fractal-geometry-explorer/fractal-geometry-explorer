Chapter 30Isothermal Surfaces of Turbulence

Where in a turbulent fluid is the temperature exactly 45°F? Topologically the answer is a collection of spheres, but no sphere of Euclid is this rough. Chapter 30 argues that the isosurfaces of temperature are fractals of dimension D = 3 − H, where Kolmogorov's theory of turbulence fixes H = 1/3 on geometric grounds alone — and with that it finally explains the fractal flakes of Plates 10 and 11 at the front of the book.

Chapter 30, the concept

Isotherms in a planar section

T is a fractional Brown function of (x, y, z) · isosurface D = 3 − H · its planar isotherms D = 2 − H

The chapter models temperature in a fluid as a fractional Brown function of the three space coordinates. Burgers' crude postulate — the mean square difference of temperature between two points grows like their distance — gives H = 1/2. Kolmogorov's dimensional argument, "a striking triumph of abstract a priori thought", corrects the growth to the 2/3 power, giving H = 1/3: antipersistent, while Earth's relief favours persistence. Cut the fluid by a plane and the isotherm T = T₀ is a curve "identical to the coastlines studied in Chapter 28, except for the value of D": here that dimension is 2 − H. The full isosurface in space has D = 3 − H, so Kolmogorov turbulence wraps its 45°F surface into a fractal of dimension 8/3.

Chapter 30, the sections

A linear section and its zero-set

temperature along a line: a fractional Brown line-to-line function, D = 2 − H · the set where T = T₀: D = 1 − H

Each cut of the model by a line gives a one-dimensional record of temperature: a fractional Brown function whose graph has dimension 2 − H. The isotherm of the cut — the instants where the record crosses T₀ — is a dust of dimension 1 − H, the pattern Chapter 8 met as a Lévy dust on the line. Antipersistence (H < 1/2) makes the record cross its level far more often than Brownian chance: the crossings in Kolmogorov turbulence (H = 1/3) form a dust of dimension 2/3, against 1/2 for Burgers.
