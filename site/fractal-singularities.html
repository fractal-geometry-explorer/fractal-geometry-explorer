The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 11, "Fractal Singularities of Differential Equations" · pages 106–108

Chapter 11Fractal Singularities

Turbulence theory is split in two: Kolmogorov's phenomenology works but does not follow from the equations of fluid motion, and the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations resist every attempt to extract turbulence from them. Mandelbrot's conjecture (1976c) bridges the gap at its sharpest point — the solutions' singularities, he contends, are not the standard points, curves and surfaces but locally scaling fractal sets. This chapter prints no plates, so every figure here is this page's own illustration of its argument.

Pages 106–107

Stringing singularities together

standard singularities: points, curves and surfaces · the conjecture: locally scaling fractal sets

How is an equation of mathematical physics solved in practice? One draws up a list of elementary "singularities" characteristic of the problem, then handles complex instances "by identifying the appropriate singularities and stringing them together" — the way a calculus student graphs a rational function from its poles. The standard singularities are points, curves and surfaces, and Mandelbrot reads the failure to derive turbulence from the Euler and Navier–Stokes equations as proof that no standard singularity fits. His conjecture: the turbulent solutions involve singularities of a new kind — locally scaling fractal sets — and near-singularities, their smoothed approximations.

Scale-free equations, scaleless singularities

the Euler equations have no built-in length · their singularities should survive a zoom unchanged

The motivation is "the very old notion that the symmetries and other invariances present in an equation 'ought' to be reflected in the equation's solution." The Euler equations are scale-free — no term singles out a length — so their typical solutions, and any singularities those solutions possess, should be scale-free too; away from walls a "principle of not feeling the boundary" should make them locally scaleless. The test: zoom in, centred on the set. A point, a line or a plane passes — those are the standard scale-free sets, and they have been tried and found wanting. A smooth curved set fails: its curvature is a built-in scale, and magnification irons it flat. What remains that still passes the test is a fractal.

Page 107

Chorin's vortex calculation: D ≈ 2.5

stretched vorticity collects on a body of dimension D ≈ 2.5 · its correction B = 0.17 ± 0.03 · (3 − D)/3 at D = 2.5 is exactly 1/6

By 1981 the conjecture had numbers on its side. Alexandre Chorin applied a vortex method to the inertial range of fully developed turbulence and found the highly stretched vorticity collecting itself "into a body of decreasing volume, and of dimension D ∼ 2.5" — squarely in chapter 10's measured range. His correction to the Kolmogorov exponents, B = 0.17 ± 0.03, is compatible with experiment, and sits almost exactly on the curdling line B = (3 − D)/3, which gives 1/6 ≈ 0.167 at D = 2.5. The same calculations suggest that solutions of Euler's equations in three dimensions blow up in a finite time — singularities for real, not only in the limit.

Viscosity, near-singularities, and Scheffer's bounds

Navier–Stokes singular times: dimension at most 1/2 · their set in space: dimension at most 1 · viscosity smooths Euler's singularities into peaks

For viscous fluids the contention sharpens: the singularities of the Navier–Stokes solutions "can only be fractals", smoother than Euler's — so of lower dimension — and the peaks of dissipation seen as intermittency are "Euler singularities smoothed out by viscosity". V. Scheffer, examining a fluid started with finite kinetic energy, proved that if singularities exist at all, their projection on the time axis has fractal dimension at most 1/2, and their set in space dimension at most 1. The time bound restates an inequality in Leray's famous 1934 paper — nearly useless for forty years, Mandelbrot argues, until the fractal reading "placed it in proper perspective".
