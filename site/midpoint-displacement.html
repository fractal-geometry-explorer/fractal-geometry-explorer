Plate 246Random Midpoint Displacement

Build a curve by bending: take each side of the current approximation and displace its midpoint, at random, by just the right amount. For Brownian motion the recipe is exact, and it is so simple and cheap that generations of computer artists adopted it. Mandelbrot's warning in this chapter: adapted to dimensions below 2 it is subtly wrong — the halving points leave permanent creases, and the increments are not stationary. The chapter's one plate is something else entirely: a gloriously broken program.

Chapter 26, pages 244–245

Random Koch curves with a time grid

N = 2 pieces of r = 2−1/D · displacement² = 2−2(k+1)/D − 2−2(k/D+1) · H = 1/D

Whenever a Koch generator is two intervals of length 2−1/D, the whole cascade is one rule: displace the midpoint of every side, orthogonally, by the amount the formula above prescribes — the construction itself tells whether to bend left or right. To randomize, "the displacement's direction is made random and isotropic" and its length Gaussian, with the formula giving the mean square. For D = 2 the result is exactly Brownian motion; for D < 2 it is the curve this chapter calls B*H(t), with H = 1/D.

Chapter 26, page 245

Nonstationary increments: the creases

⟨ΔX²⟩ = Δt2H on dyadic intervals only · elsewhere smaller, ≥ 21−2HΔt2H · the creases never heal

Watch one coordinate X(t) of the curve. Over a dyadic interval — from h2−k to (h+1)2−k — the mean square increment is exactly Δt2H. Slide the same window half a step and the variance drops: the window now straddles a point where two independently built halves merely meet. Those points are creases. "With D barely below 2 … the creases are slight. However, with H nearly 1 … the creases are very important and can be seen on the sample functions." The only cure, says the book, is to give up the recursive scheme.

The numbers are measured on this page: 4,000 fresh curves, mean square increment over all windows of length 1/32, dyadic against half-slid. On dyadic windows the construction gives Δt2H exactly; on slid ones the measured value falls between the book's lower bound 21−2HΔt2H and Δt2H, and only H = 1/2 makes the two agree — stationary increments. The layers view shows why: every stage's contribution vanishes on the same dyadic grid, "in synchrony with all the following ones".

Chapter 26, pages 245–246

Randomly positioned strata

stratum k: a broken line through Poisson instants, bk per unit time · the sum BH†(t) is stationary · b → 1 gives BH(t)

The creases trace back to hierarchy: every stage of the midpoint scheme hangs its tents on the same dyadic grid. So destroy the grid. Let stratum k be a broken line interpolating independent random values at Poisson instants — on average bk of them per unit time — and add the strata up. No instant is special any more, so the sum BH†(t) is a stationary random function (Ditlevsen 1969). The price: it is self-similar only by the discrete ratios b−k. As b → 1 the admissible ratios crowd together, but BH† "loses its specificity" and converges to the fractional Brownian motion BH(t) of the next chapter.

The computer "bug" as artist, opus 1

a very strict order had been designed in · "here, this order is hidden, and no other order is apparent"

The plate "can be credited in part to faulty computer programming": a program meant to draw random round cut-outs — the design that, once "the bug was promptly identified and corrected", became Plates 306 to 309 — misdrew them, and the result went "well beyond anything we had expected". Mandelbrot kept the accident, numbered it like any plate, and noted that it "could pass for High Art". The printed picture is a dense starburst of streaks shot through with round dark holes.

What the figure shows instead: the strict order the program was meant to draw (a field of round tremas, the design of Plates 306–309, which belong to chapter 33), and a bug of our own invention — a pen that lags behind its ray while the picture is retraced from the centre — chosen because it, too, turns neat circles into a radial burst.
