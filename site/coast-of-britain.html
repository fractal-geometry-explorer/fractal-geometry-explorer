The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 5, "How Long Is the Coast of Britain?" · pages 25–33

Plate 33How Long Is the Coast of Britain?

Set dividers to an opening ε and walk them along a coastline: the number of steps times ε is an approximate length L(ε). Shrink ε and, instead of settling toward a true length, L(ε) grows without limit — every bay hides subbays, every peninsula subpeninsulas. Richardson's measurements put a law on the growth, L(ε) ∝ ε1−D, and Mandelbrot's reading of the exponent founded this book: D is a dimension, and it exceeds 1.

Plate 33

Richardson's empirical data on the rate of increase of coastlines' lengths

L(ε) ≈ F·ε1−D · each line's slope estimates 1 − D · from Richardson 1961

Lewis Fry Richardson — meteorologist, pioneer of turbulence theory, student of armed conflict — measured map after map with equal-sided polygons of ever shorter side ε. The diagrams, found among his papers after he died, were published in a near-confidential yearbook. On doubly logarithmic paper every coastline and land frontier falls on a straight line of negative slope: there are two constants F and D with L(ε) ≈ F·ε1−D. The circle alone behaves the way school geometry promises, stabilising rapidly near its circumference. To Richardson the slopes had no theoretical interpretation; Mandelbrot (1967) read each as 1 − D, with D a fractal dimension.

The 39 markers were digitised from the scan at 600 dpi, each centre good to about 0.005 of a decade; the grid itself is hand-drawn, with a row pitch uneven by 5%, so values are interpolated rule to rule. West Britain's printed line has slope −0.261 (D ≈ 1.26, the caption's −0.25); Australia −0.129 and South Africa −0.006, against the −0.13 and −0.02 usually quoted from Richardson. The German frontier prints three of its yardsticks twice, two measurements each (plotted as printed), and the one open ring among the circle's dots is read here as a circle measurement. Spain claims 987 km for its frontier with Portugal, Portugal 1214 km; the Netherlands 380 km against Belgium's 449. With slope −0.25, a factor of 2 in ε accounts for the 20% — and the small country, mapping more finely, measures more.

Pages 25–27

Walking the dividers along a coastline

L(ε) ≈ F·ε1−D · Quadric coast D = log 8/log 4 = 3/2

The chapter's Method A, run honestly: set the dividers to ε, walk them along the curve planting a vertex every ε, and multiply steps by ε. A model coastline stands in for Britain — a quadric Koch curve of dimension 3/2, the value the chapter announces as a first approximation for coastlines (too large for real coasts, but enough to show D > 1 is natural). The book's other methods smooth instead of stepping: Method B walks a man a distance ε from the water, Method C covers the coast with Cantor's out-of-focus blotches — the ε-sausage of Plate 32 — and Method D covers it with the fewest blotches possible. All misbehave identically, and each later became a definition of dimension (Cantor–Minkowski–Bouligand, Pontrjagin–Schnirelman).

The teragon is carried to a stage whose edges are far shorter than ε, so the red polygon is a genuine divider walk, and the last partial chord counts as a fraction of a step. The fitted slope wobbles around 1 − D rather than equalling it — the measured length of a Koch curve oscillates log-periodically with the yardstick, a real effect of divider walks, not an error. Steinhaus saw the phenomenon in 1954 on the left bank of the Vistula ("lengths ten, hundred or even thousand times as great as the length read off the school map"); Kasner used it to tease small tots about the east coast of the United States. On a tamed coast — Chelsea's, say — L(ε) flattens between about 20 m and 20 cm, and a Coast Guard could legislate an ε inside the flat zone; Nature, existing apart from Man, accepts no such decree.

Pages 30–31

Hausdorff's measure, and why D is a dimension

Approximate measure = N(ε)·εd ≈ F·εd−D · finite and nonzero only at d = D

Why call D a dimension? Hausdorff's idea: the linear measure of a polygon adds its sides' lengths — each raised, one may say, to the power d = 1; the surface measure of its interior paves it with squares and adds the sides raised to d = 2. Use the "wrong" power and the answer says nothing: every polygon's area is zero, its interior's length infinite. Now take the coastline's approximating polygon of N(ε) sides of length ε and raise them to the power d. Since N(ε) ≈ F·ε−D, the sum N(ε)·εd behaves reasonably if and only if d = D: below D it blows up, above D it dies to zero. An infinite coastline is four times longer than each of its fourths — useless; but its measure in dimension D is finite, and four times that of each fourth.

The curve plots the actual divider walks of the section above, so its slope is measured, not assumed. D is a fraction — that is the odd part — and it exceeds 1, which is rigorously the topological dimension of a curve. Mandelbrot's proposal: call a curve fractal when its fractal dimension exceeds its topological dimension. The chapter's summary sentence is its manifesto: "Coastlines are fractal patterns." The step of explaining coastlines — deducing D from how relief forms — waits for chapter 28's Brown surfaces, and the simpler similarity dimension for the next chapter.
