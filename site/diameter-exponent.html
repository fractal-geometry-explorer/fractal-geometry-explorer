Plates 163–165Trees and the Diameter Exponent

Lungs, arteries, botanical trees and rivers are trees with thick stems, and trees with thick stems cannot be self-similar. The best one can hope is that self-similarity holds for the branch tips. Beside the tips' dimension D, such a tree carries a second number: the diameter exponent Δ, which says how fast the branches thin out. Leonardo da Vinci already knew one of its values. Pages 156–162

The diameter exponent Δ

dΔ = d₁Δ + d₂Δ at every bifurcation · lung Δ ≈ 3 · arteries Δ ≈ 2.7 · botanical trees and rivers Δ = 2 · neurons Δ ≈ 1.5

Leonardo wrote that "all the branches of a tree at every stage of its height when put together are equal in thickness to the trunk below them". In formulas: at a bifurcation the diameters obey dΔ = d₁Δ + d₂Δ with Δ = 2, since cross-sections go as d². Mandelbrot measures other anatomies by the same exponent. In the lung the air slows down toward thinner bronchi, and resistance arguments (Murray's) and space-packing arguments both land on Δ = 3. For rivers, the discharge Q is preserved at junctions and width goes as √Q, so Leonardo's Δ = 2 holds for streams too, as he himself suspected.

The figure is a diagram, not a plate: the book draws no figure for these pages. Δ only fixes how diameters shrink; the angles and lengths here are decorative. At Δ = 2 the total cross-section is kept at every split, so a tree is a bundle of constant-width vessels — the "pipe model" of botanists. Above 2 the total section grows and the flow slows; the lung's Δ ≈ 3 makes the branching ratio 21/3 ≈ 1.26, the value Murray's least-resistance argument asks for.

Plates 164 and 165

Plane-filling recursive bronchi

Two subtrees in ratios r₁ and r₂ · tips' D solves r₁D + r₂D = 1 · diameters d = c·ℓ

A trunk with barren sides and one fertile bud grows two branches at right angles, one in ratio r₁, the other in r₂; each branch repeats the rule, forever. The whole tree is not self-similar — it keeps a trunk as residue — but the set of branch tips is, with dimension D solving r₁D + r₂D = 1. In Plate 165 each branch is a rectangle whose width is a fixed fraction of its length, and the tree fills a roughly rectangular piece of the plane with no gap and no overlap. In Plate 164 the width-to-length ratio shrinks at every stage — faster in the left half than in the right — and as the branches thin, the tips' dimension can reach D = 2.

The construction was decoded from the scans: branches meet in a T whose two bars tuck behind the parent's tip, the long branch flips which side its own long daughter takes while the short branch keeps it, and the printed figures follow this rule branch for branch. The ratios are fitted: r₁ ≈ 0.718, r₂ ≈ 0.678, width 0.15 of length for the top of Plate 165; r₁ ≈ 0.726, r₂ ≈ 0.582, width 0.41 for the bottom. Plate 164's per-stage width schedule is our reconstruction (widths fitted as shrinking by ≈ 0.66 per stage on the left and ≈ 0.78 on the right); its large-scale structure matches the plate, the finest twigs only approximately. When the width-to-length ratio is fixed, Δ = D; when widths shrink faster, Δ < D — the chapter's two cases.

Plate 163

Flattened fractal models of plant flowers

An umbrella tree of sticks, every stick thickened into triangles · angles θ/2 at the root, π−θ at the tip · Δ = D here

Take an umbrella tree of infinitely thin sticks, as in Plate 155: two sticks from the root with the angle θ between them, and every stick forking into two daughters at the same angle, r times shorter. Now thicken every stick into triangles that have the stick as a side, with the angle θ/2 at the stick's root and π−θ at its tip. Around every fork the corners of the triangles add up to exactly 360°, so when θ is as small as self-avoidance allows, the thickened stems neither overlap nor leave gaps: they fill the umbrella's insides, and the figure goes solid, scalloped like a cauliflower. The branches thin out most when D comes close to 1 or 2 — Mandelbrot asks whether the D's of real flowers sit where branches are thickest.
