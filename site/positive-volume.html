The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 15, "Surfaces with Positive Volume, and Flesh" · pages 147–150

Chapter 15Surfaces of Positive Volume

Part V's opening chapter builds the mildest-sounding monsters in the book: dusts with positive length, curves with positive area, surfaces with positive volume. The trick is a trema ratio that shrinks from stage to stage. The payoff is anatomy — tissue in which an artery and a vein run arbitrarily close to every point is exactly such a set. The chapter prints no plates, so every figure below is this page's illustration of its constructions.

Chapter 15, pages 147–148

Cantor dusts of positive measure

λₖ = λ₁qᵏ⁻¹ · remaining measure Π(1 − λₖ) > 0 when Σλₖ < ∞ · D_T = 0, D = 1

Cantor's triadic dust has zero length because its tremas keep a fixed ratio: cut the middle λ out of every interval forever and the remaining length Π(1 − λ)ᴷ dies. But the dust's other properties — totally disconnected, D_T = 0 — come only from the bisection, not the ratio. Let the ratio λₖ shrink with the stage k, fast enough that Σλₖ converges, and the remainder keeps a positive length 1 − P while staying a dust. It is no longer self-similar, so it has no similarity dimension, but Hausdorff's definition answers D = 1: a fractal still, since D > D_T, whose D and D_T describe it "very superficially". The same cross-cutting in the plane leaves a dust of positive area with D = 2, and in space a dust of positive volume with D = 3.

The chapter prints no figure, so the bar (drawn in Plate 80's style), the plane dust and the charts are this page's illustrations. The λₖ = λ₁qᵏ⁻¹ schedule is this page's choice of a summable sequence; the chapter only asks Σλₖ < ∞. The drifting formal dimension Dₖ = log 2/log(1/rₖ) with rₖ = (1 − λₖ)/2 is the chapter's own device: it climbs toward 1 as the tremas fade, embodying the effective dimension of Chapter 3's ball of thread. The three classes of linear dust the chapter sorts out — D < 1, D = 1 with zero length, D = 1 with positive length — appear as q < 1 against q = 1 here (D = 1 with zero length needs λₖ → 0 with Σλₖ = ∞, as in λₖ = 1/k, which the presets skip).

Chapter 15, pages 148–149

A curve of positive area

marshland area Π(1 − λₖ) · the limit: a curve, D_T = 1, with D = 2 and positive area

Dusts of positive volume don't offend intuition much — but after Lebesgue 1903 and Osgood 1903, curves of positive area had to be swallowed too. The chapter generalizes the midpoint construction of Plate 43: keep cutting bays and promontories out of a strip of marshland, but let their relative widths λₖ tend to 0 with Π(1 − λₖ) > 0. The marshland then never loses its area — yet in the limit it is a genuine curve: no interior points, and D_T = 1, because any point can be cut off from the rest by removing just two points.

A reconstruction in kind: the chapter describes Osgood's bays and promontories in words and prints nothing, so this page draws the simplest scheme with the same bookkeeping — Knopp's wedge removal, in which each triangle sheds a wedge of relative area exactly λₖ and splits in two, and the curve is the limit of the triangle chains. The area is then exactly Π(1 − λₖ) at every stage, the property the chapter's construction is built to have. At q = 1 the same picture collapses to an ordinary zero-area Koch-like curve. The chapter adds a remark run the other way: a Koch curve whose generator grows at every stage can drive D to 2 while the area stays 0 — a plane-filling curve's D without its double points, met again in the rivers that "crisscross finer scale domains less thoroughly" below their inner cutoff.

Chapter 15, pages 149–150

Arteries, veins and flesh

tissue: D_T = 2, D = 3, most of the volume · vessels: standard domains, a small share

Harvey's circulation puts an artery and a vein within a small distance of nearly every point of the body; blood being expensive, the vessels must still occupy only a small share of the volume. Euclid finds the demands contradictory: tissue must be the common boundary of two three-dimensional networks, yet have far more volume than both. The spatial Osgood construction delivers precisely this. Vessels are standard domains — blood cells fit inside them — while tissue contains no piece, however small, free of both networks: a fractal surface with D_T = 2 and D = 3. "Lebesgue-Osgood monsters are the very substance of our flesh!"

A schematic planar section, this page's own: each stage cuts a cross — artery at odd stages, vein at even — of relative area λₖ from every tile, so the tissue keeps the share Π(1 − λₖ) while every tissue point has a stage-k artery and a stage-(k+1) vein within the size of its stage-k tile (the "within" readouts). What the section cannot show: in the genuine spatial model each network connects into one branching tree, the tissue is a connected surface (D_T = 2), and Brouwer's construction even gives arteries, veins and the lung's bronchioles one common boundary — the chapter's answer to Hahn's claim that such sets put geometry beyond intuition. The flat tiles here stand for sections of tissue, not for dust.

Of intuition, old and new

The chapter closes on Hans Hahn's essay against geometric intuition, which offers the three-domain boundary as proof that intuition "is a wholly unreliable guide" and must yield to logic. Mandelbrot's verdict is the opposite: once the monsters are seen doing honest work — here, literally as flesh — intuition does not break, it trains. The Essay's recurring moral: "mathematics is too important to be abandoned to fanatic logicians."
