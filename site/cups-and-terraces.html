Chapter 29Islands, Lakes and Cups

The Brown relief of chapter 28, put to the test of areas. Islands pass: Korčak's hyperbolic law Pr(A > a) = Fa⁻ᴮ falls out of the model with B = D/2. Lakes fail — they are not the mirror image of islands — and chasing why leads to two lake surrogates, deadvalleys and cups, to terraces fit for the Devil, and to an eroded Brown model. The chapter prints no figures, so everything below is this page's illustration of its arguments.

Chapter 29, page 272

Korčak's law of island areas

Pr(A > a) = Fa⁻ᴮ · B = D/2 = (2 − H)/2 · H = 0.8 fits "all of Earth"

The scatter of island sizes is often the most striking thing on a map. Korčak 1938 found it hyperbolic: the number of islands larger than a falls like a⁻ᴮ. Chapter 13 derived exactly this for nonrandom Koch islands with B = D/2; here the relation survives when the coastline is a level set of a random relief — it is enough that the relief be self-similar. For the fractional Brown relief the coastline dimension is D = 2 − H, so B = 1 − H/2, and the persistence H = 0.8 (B = 0.6) comes very close to the empirical distribution for the whole Earth.

Chapter 29, pages 272–273

Islands against deadvalleys

same exponent B, different prefactor F · a deadvalley: land enclosing ground below sea level

Lakes look like anti-islands, but the definitions refuse the symmetry. The honest mirror image of an ocean island is a deadvalley: an area enclosed by the continent whose ground lies below ocean level — the basins of the Dead Sea, the Caspian, Death Valley, the Qattara Depression, some holding water and some dry. Deadvalleys obey the same exponent B as islands, so the 10th and 20th largest of each stand in the same ratio; what differs is Korčak's prefactor F, which sets the absolute sizes. On a map that is mostly ocean, islands get the greater F; make the map mostly land and the advantage flips to the deadvalleys.

This page's own reading of the chapter's map-inspection argument, on one H = 0.8 field: an island is a connected patch above sea level that is not the main landmass (the component touching most border, or the largest); a deadvalley is a below-level patch enclosed by land, i.e. not connected to the ocean. Lakes are a third thing entirely — their bottoms need not lie below z = 0 and their surfaces need not lie at z = 0 — and the chapter concedes that their hyperbolic law may be mere robustness of the distribution under "diverse forms of torture": a hyperbolic primeval relief times a thousand arbitrary geological multipliers stays hyperbolic.

Chapter 29, pages 273–276

Cups and the Devil's terraces

fill every dip to the brim · B*(x): a Lévy staircase up, then down · cup lengths hyperbolic, exponent H

Make the landscape impermeable and fill every dip exactly to its brim: the filled dips are the cups. A droplet landing above the filled surface B* escapes to the ocean along a never-ascending path; a droplet with B < z < B* is trapped forever. On a line, B* is explicit — the running maximum up to the summit, then the running maximum from the far end — a Lévy devil's staircase climbing and then descending, continuous, varying on a set of length zero. Mandelbrot conjectures a random droplet is almost certain to land in a cup: the cups' surfaces are then "a wild extrapolate of the terraced fields in southeast Asia" — the Devil's terraces — and their cumulative coastline a ramified net, a random kin of the Sierpiński gasket.

The map fills the two-dimensional field by priority flood, the exact two-dimensional B*: every cell rises to the lowest brim on any path to the border. A cut across that map (the third mode) lies below the cut's own one-dimensional filling — more escape routes, less water — and is the "devilishly terraced" profile of the text, peaked maxima over flat floors. The dirt readout is the chapter's thrift argument: with D barely above 2 the fill volume Σ(relative area)^(1+H/2) is small, so erosion can level the cups without reshaping the hills — the eroded model B*, scaling again if the ocean is set right, with dimension between 2 and 3 − H. What altitude destroys is drawn too: watershed points are maxima and passes, river points never are, and a Brown relief has local minima — so it has no river trees, "a fresh strike" against the model, patched by the filling.
