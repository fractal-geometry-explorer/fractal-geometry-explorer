The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 20, "Fractal Attractors and Fractal ('Chaotic') Evolutions" · pages 193–199

Plates 198–199Attraction to Fractals

A ball dropped in a funnel wiggles, settles, and stops at the tip: the tip is an attractor. Henri Poincaré observed long ago that the orbits of nonlinear dynamical systems may be "attracted" to far odder sets — sets Mandelbrot identifies as nonlinear fractals. This chapter turns that observation around: it contrives dynamical systems whose attractors are fractals already tamed in Chapters 18 and 19, so that a plotted orbit draws the fractal. Both plates were made this way, 64,000 dots at a time.

Pages 194–195

An attractor that is a dust

x → λx(1−x) · attractors over λ · at λ∞ = 3.5699… the attractor is a fractal dust, D ≈ 0.538

For each λ, iterate x → λx(1−x) and throw away the early steps; what the orbit keeps visiting is the attractor. First a single point, then — each point "bifurcates into 2" — a cycle of 2, 4, 8… The cascade accumulates at λ∞ ≈ 3.5699, and there, after "an infinity of bifurcations, this attractor is a fractal dust" with D ≈ 0.538: a kin of the Cantor dust, but with two unequal similarity ratios, one of them Feigenbaum's 1/α ≈ 0.3995. Beyond it the evolution on the attractor looks "chaotic": no point is visited twice in finite time.

The "magnify" button re-centres the window on (λ∞, ½) and shrinks it by δ ≈ 4.669 horizontally and α ≈ 2.503 vertically — Mandelbrot's point that the tree, juxtaposing the attractors in the (x, λ) plane, is asymptotically self-affine: successive windows nearly repeat (each is also mirrored, since α acts with a sign — the drawing flips odd steps back). Since δ ≠ α the tree is self-affine, not self-similar, and only "with a residue": the windows agree better and better but never exactly. D ≈ 0.538 is Grassberger's value, which the book cites from a preprint.

Plate 198

The San Marco dragon, drawn by attraction

the two inverses of x → 3x(1−x) · z = ½ ± √(¼ − z/3) · a pseudo-random index picks one per step

Chapter 19's San Marco dragon — the self-squared curve of λ = 3 — reappears here as an attractor. Run forward, x → 3x(1−x) repels from the dragon's skin; so "making time run backward", the system iterates the map's two inverses, and under wide conditions the orbit is attracted onto the curve. Which inverse to take at each step is the "determining index", chosen among 2 possibilities by a pseudo-random algorithm repeated 64,000 times; the first few positions are not plotted. The dots do not land uniformly: in the book's words, "regions in the neighborhoods of cusps and self-intersections are very slow to fill" — watch the pinch points stay sparse while the bulbs blacken.

Plate 199

The Pharaoh's Breastplate

four inversions, "selected to insure that the limit set 𝓛 is a collection of circles" · determining index among 4

The other shape drawn by attraction is self-inverse (Chapter 18): the limit set of a group of four circle inversions, chosen so that the limit set degenerates from a wild curve into "a collection of circles" — infinitely many, nested in a crescent between two tangent circles, every one of them kissing its neighbours. The plate again plots a long orbit: at each of 64,000 steps a pseudo-random index picks one of the 4 inversions, and the moving point is attracted onto the necklace. The K buttons below play one such step on the whole figure at once: everything streams through the chosen circle, and the necklace lands exactly on itself — which is what self-inverse means. Mandelbrot later named the figure the Pharaoh's Breastplate for its likeness to Tutankhamun's jewellery.

The book nowhere states the four inversions, so they are this page's reconstruction, decoded from the scan (the derivation is logged in the repository). The two bounding circles are tangent at the top; seen from that tangency point the crescent straightens into a strip, and every tangency in the print forces the inversions' positions exactly — two circles of radius 1/√2 seated on the strip's edges, and two mirrors, one half-period either side of them (the necklace repeats with period 1/√2; in the crescent all four become the dashed circles above). The K buttons' motion is a flow on the Riemann sphere that has the acting circle as its equator: inversion is the mirror through the equator's plane, and each point slides along its meridian to the mirrored latitude. The halftime pinch, when the whole figure crosses the equator — the acting circle — at once, is genuine: that circle is the only thing the inversion holds fixed. One number is left free, the crescent's radius ratio: 0.647 is fitted to the plate (drag the slider for the rest of the family — the same limit set seen through different Möbius frames). Checked at 600 dpi, points generated this way sit a median 4 px from the plate's ink, against 35 px for random points in the crescent. The dots' texture still depends on the book's unknowable random sequence and exact generators, so it is matched only statistically — like the print, this run fills the horns and the pinch points last; the circles mode is exact. The circle-count exponent e is this page's own measurement, the same census Chapter 18 runs on Apollonian nets.
