Plates 68–73Sweeps, Rivers and Trees

Peano curves are far from being monsters with no concrete meaning. When they avoid touching themselves, the two sides of the curve form two interlocking trees, rivers and watersheds, which are good first models of drainage basins, botanical trees and blood vessels. These plates show sweeps of the Koch snowflake and of Gosper's island, their trees, and trees grown directly from tree-shaped generators. Every figure is computed from the rules in the captions.

The snowflake sweeps

Seven intervals: six of length 1/3, one of 1/√3 · 6·(1/3)² + (1/√3)² = 1 · D = 2 · rivers D = log 4/log 3 ≈ 1.2619

A family of Peano curves Mandelbrot designed. They fill the original Koch snowflake of Plate 45, bringing together two monsters of around 1900. The initiator is [0, 1], and the curve fills the snowflake built on the equilateral triangle with that base. The generator, called S for straight, has six intervals of length 1/3 and one of length 1/√3. Its mirror image in the line x = ½ is F, for flipped. Either may be used at any stage, so each sequence of S and F gives a different snowflake sweep.

Plates 70 and 71

The Peano–Gosper curve and its trees

N = 7, r = 1/√7, D = 2 · its boundary, and every river and watershed, D = log 3/log √7 ≈ 1.1291

The thin broken lines of Plate 46 were the first stages of a curve due to Gosper (Gardner 1976). It was the first Peano curve that avoids touching itself to be obtained by the Koch method without further processing. Turned until its first link is horizontal, its generator lies on a triangular lattice, where it uses 7 of the 3 × 7 links. The curve fills the Gosper island of Plate 46.

Plates 72 and 73

Plane-filling trees and the fudgeflake

A Y-shaped generator: three links of 1/√3 · N = 3, D = 2 · each river D = log 2/log √3 = log 4/log 3

The river trees deduced from Peano curves can also be grown directly, from a generator that is itself a tree. Take [0, 1] as initiator and three links of length 1/√3 joining the corners of the equilateral triangle on [0, 1] to its centre. Each river is a midpoint displacement curve like Plate 43's, of dimension log 4/log 3. To leave room for the rivers, the generator must alternate between the right and the left. That fudges the snowflake's symmetry, and the domain these rivers drain is called the fudgeflake.

Plate 73, bottom, and Plate 49

The quartet

Five intervals of length 1/√5 · N = 5, D = 2 · boundary D = log 3/log √5 ≈ 1.3652

A different Koch curve, together with three curves that fill it: one Peano curve and two trees. The Peano curve on [0, 1] uses five intervals of length 1/√5. Its boundary converges to a Koch curve of dimension log 3/log √5 ≈ 1.3652. Mandelbrot calls the shape the quartet; it is the figure in the centre of Plate 49. Each "player", and the table between them, pertile: they are the five pieces the generator's five intervals sweep.
