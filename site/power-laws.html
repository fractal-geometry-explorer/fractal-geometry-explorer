Hyperbolic distributions and the Lindy effect

Pr(U > u) = Fu⁻ᴰ · past any u₀ the tail is the whole law rescaled · E(U | U > u₀) = u₀·D/(D − 1)

A random variable U is hyperbolic when Pr(U > u) = Fu−D. Its defining habit is scaling: condition on having passed any threshold u₀ and the distribution beyond is the whole law all over again, only rescaled. When D > 1 the conditional expectation is u₀·D/(D − 1) — a result that "suggests endless paradoxical stories": the Lindy effect ("the future career expectation of a television comedian is proportional to his past exposure"), the young poets' cemetery, the receding shore.

Zipf's law of word frequencies

P = F(ρ + V)^(−1/D) · D = log N/log(1/r) · V = 1/(N − 1)

Rank the words of one speaker's discourse by decreasing frequency. Zipf's empirical law says the probability of the word of rank ρ is near-universally P ∝ 1/ρ — and in Mandelbrot's second approximation, his first published result, P = F(ρ + V)−1/D. The explanation needs no linguistics: let discourse be a stream of independent letters — N proper letters of probability r each, the "improper letter" space with the remainder 1 − Nr — and the lexicographic tree of all words is scaling: every branch is a reduced copy of the whole tree. The law drops out, with D = log N/log(1/r) measuring the richness of vocabulary.

The identification of the word set with a Cantor-like dust of dimension D is the book's; the tree drawing generalizes its base-(N + 1) description to arbitrary r by giving each letter a share r, which reduces to the book's digits when r = 1/(N + 1).

The temperature of discourse

The deviations from the classical case allow a second reading, patterned after statistical thermodynamics: the cost of coding a word plays energy, Shannon's information plays entropy, and D comes out as the "temperature of discourse" — the hotter the discourse, the higher the probability of rare words. The standard case is D < 1, where the dictionary may be infinite. But rare, unquestionable cases show D > 1, and then the series Σ(ρ + V)−1/D diverges: ΣP = 1 can only hold if the dictionary is finite. In the book's data this happens only where the vocabulary is artificially limited from outside — Latin inserts in a non-Latin text — and since a finite set of points is no fractal, this D is not a fractal dimension.

Mandelbrot's dichotomy came first as language statistics; its physical counterpart was recognized independently by Norman Ramsey — a body hotter than "infinitely hot" needs a negative inverse temperature, which likewise demands a finite upper bound on energy (see Mandelbrot 1970p for the parallelism).

The Pareto law for salaries

level k: N^k members at pay r^k · D = log N/log(1/r) · empirically D ≈ 2

Pareto found that the number of people with income above u falls off like u−D. The chapter's scaling model (after Lydall 1959) is another abstract tree: the organization chart of a hierarchy in which every member has N subordinates, each paid r times their superior. Level k then holds Nk members earning rk, and "the formalism used for Zipf's law applies without change" — with a D that "does not seem to have any fractal interpretation worth writing down". The greater D, the lower the inequality; empirically D is ordinarily near 2.

Lydall's model has a catch the book points out: at D = 2 the smallest possible 1/r, reached at N = 2, is √2 — a 41% raise per level, unrealistically high. So within real hierarchies D > 2, and a population's overall D ≈ 2 must come mostly from differences between hierarchies.
