The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 8, "Fractal Events and Cantor Dusts" · pages 74–83

Plates 80–83Cantor Dust

On a data line, errors come in bursts, the bursts in smaller bursts, and between them lie error-free gaps at every scale. Mandelbrot's rough model of this intermittency is Cantor's dust: start with an interval and cut out middle thirds forever. What is left is a "dust" of points — too thin to draw, so the plates thicken it into a bar, hang it in curtains, and pile up its mass into the devil's staircase. Every figure is computed from the rules in the captions.

Plate 80

Cantorian triadic curdling: the Cantor bar

N = 2, r = 1/3 · D = log 2/log 3 ≈ 0.6309 · each stage: 2× the slugs, 1/3 the length, 3/2 the density

The dust itself is "thin and spare to the point of being invisible," so the plate thickens it into a Cantor bar. Start with a round bar of very low density, seen in projection as a long rectangle. Matter "curdles" out of the bar's middle third into the end thirds, whose positions do not change; then out of each of their middle thirds, and so on. The limit is infinitely many infinitely thin slugs of infinitely high density, spaced along the line as the Cantor dust.

Plate 81

Cantor curtains: every cut a dust

N = 2, r from 0 to 1/2 · every horizontal cut is a Cantor dust · D = log 2/log (1/r), from 0 to 1

Here the Cantor dust is "clarified by being placed among generalized dusts with N = 2 and variable r." Both theater curtains are topped by the full interval [0, 1]. Going down, r shrinks and the middle tremas open wider, so the ink gathers into hanging folds. Every horizontal cut of either figure is some Cantor dust; the arrows point out the triadic one, r = 1/3, D ≈ 0.6309. The two figures differ only in the vertical scale: the top one is linear in the dimension D, the bottom one linear in the ratio r.

Plate 83

The Cantor function, or devil's staircase

length of the graph = 2, so D = 1 · the risers' abscissas form the dust, D ≈ 0.6309 · rises on a set of length 0

The Cantor function describes how mass is spread along the Cantor bar of Plate 80. Give the bar length 1 and mass 1, and let M(R) be the mass between 0 and R. Over every gap M(R) stays flat — and the gaps add up to the whole length of the bar. Yet M(R) climbs from 0 to 1, over infinitely many, infinitely small, highly clustered rises sitting on the slugs. The plate fills the unit square under the graph with ink.

Checked against the scan: the printed figure is the unit square with the region under M(R) in solid ink (the top profile matches the Cantor function to 0.5% of the height, the difference being ink spread at the near-vertical risers). The widths of the steps sum to 1 and so do the heights, and the graph's length is exactly 2 — finite, so its dimension is 1: a curve can be full of irregularity and still be rectifiable. The staircase is continuous but climbs entirely on a set of zero length; its derivative vanishes almost everywhere.

Chapter 8, pages 74–79

Cantor dust and its dimension

N pieces of ratio r, evenly spaced · D = log N / log (1/r) · any D between 0 and 1 can be reached

The errors on a transmission line gave Mandelbrot his first concrete use for fractals: bursts within bursts, separated by error-free gaps at every scale. Cantor's cascade models them. Divide an interval into b = 1/r pieces, keep N of them — the curds — and empty the rest, the tremas. Repeating forever leaves a dust of dimension D = log N/log (1/r), anywhere between 0 and 1. Counting instants of error between 0 and R gives M(R) ∝ RD, just as for the mass of a D-dimensional ball.

The book names these dusts and their D by formula but draws only the triadic one, so the evenly spaced arrangement here (first piece at 0, last ending at 1, equal tremas between) is a reconstruction — the simplest rule consistent with the text. The two dusts with D = 1/2 "look" very different even though their D is equal; chapter 34 turns that observation into lacunarity. The gap-count chart verifies the book's claim that Nr(U > u), the number of gaps longer than u, steps across a line F·u−D.
