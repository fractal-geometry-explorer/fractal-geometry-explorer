Plates 228–231Random Chains and Squigs

Chapter 23's random curdling lets every cell evolve on its own, so whether the result hangs together is left to chance. This chapter constrains the recursion so the limit is guaranteed to be a connected, self-avoiding curve. Random chains generalize Koch and Peano curves by tossing a coin between generators; squigs refine a river's valley one halving at a time, and their dimension comes out near 4/3. Plate 228

Random Koch coastline

Two generators on a square lattice · N = 18, b = 6, both · D = log 18 / log 6 ≈ 1.6131

A Koch curve with a prescribed D and no self-contact can often be built in several ways on the same grid, between the same endpoints. Whenever two different generators fit within the same overall outline, the construction is randomized by tossing a coin between them at every interval of every stage. The book's two generators each take 18 steps of length 1/6. The second is Plate 53's. In the first, six of the steps break off as a closed ring — so wherever the coin lands that way, a satellite island splits off the coast, and the islands grow their own islands.

Plate 229

Random Peano curve

N = 4, r = 1/2 · D = 2 · the sweep of a triangle, randomized

The generator takes the interval's equilateral triangle and threads its four half-size subtriangles: up the left side, across the top, down to the base's midpoint, and out. Its flipped form is the mirror image through the interval's midpoint, and visits the base point first. Every interval of every stage carries such a triangle — its shelter — and is replaced inside it by the straight or the flipped thread. Tossing a fair coin between the two at every interval is the randomization: the two focal points are symmetric with respect to the midpoint, and either choice sweeps on, so the limit fills its region with D = 2.

Plate 230

Triangle and squig curve

Links per stage: 1 (probability 1/4) or 3 (probability 3/4), mean 2.5 · D = log 2.5 / log 2 ≈ 1.3219

The simplest squig models a river burrowing into its valley. The valley is a chain of triangles, each touching the next through a shared side. To refine it, halve every shared side and toss a coin for which half the river keeps. Those choices fix the finer valley completely: inside each triangle the new chain runs through 1 of its four subtriangles when the kept halves meet at a corner, and through 3 of them otherwise. The valley narrows forever and closes down on a curve. Mandelbrot conjectured, and Peyrière proved, that its dimension is log 2.5/log 2.

Plate 231

A hexa-squig coastline

Six squigs strung into a self-avoiding loop · D ≈ 4/3

String six squigs together around a hexagon of six triangles and the chain closes into a self-avoiding loop: a coastline. Its dimension is very close to 4/3 — a value that keeps reappearing wherever self-avoidance rules, from the boundary of the Brownian hull (Plate 243) to self-avoiding random walks. That this crude construction lands on the same number is, for Mandelbrot, no coincidence, and a hint about the geometric structure of the plane.
