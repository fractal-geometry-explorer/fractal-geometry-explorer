Plates 141–146Gaskets, Webs and Sponges

The Koch curves of Chapter 6 avoid touching themselves, and the Peano curves of Chapter 7 cannot help it. This chapter takes the step between: shapes that are deliberately ramified, curves and surfaces made of nothing but branch points. Sierpiński's gasket, its spatial kin the skewed web, the carpet and the Menger sponge are fractal lattices, invariant by scaling where the standard lattices are invariant by translation. Plate 141

Sierpiński arrowhead

N = 3, r = 1/2 · D = log 3/log 2 ≈ 1.5849 · order of ramification R = 3 or 4

In Sierpiński 1915, the initiator is [0, 1] and the generator is half a hexagon: three legs of length ½ rising at 60°, running level, and falling back. Hahn listed this curve among the monsters, because every one of its points is a branch point. Mandelbrot answers with the Eiffel Tower, trusses whose members are themselves subtrusses: "the key to strength lies in branch points."

The generator's codes were read off the caption's second teragon: the outer legs carry mirrored copies. The plate is the piece of the curve between two successive returns to the midpoint of [0, 1] — turned sideways, a tropical fish. Its stage here was fitted to the plate by eye (stage 8); the black-and-white pattern of the tremas is not painted in but falls out of filling that one closed polygon. Removing 3 or 4 well-chosen points disconnects any neighbourhood in any finite approximation, which is why the gasket, alone among known shapes, is both quasi-homogeneous and self-similar.

Diagram on page 132

Critical percolation clusters

bond percolation on the square lattice · p_c = 1/2 · backbone D ≈ 1.6, close to the gasket's 1.5849

Mandelbrot's claim: the Sierpiński gasket's branching structure is a promising model of the backbone of a critical percolation cluster. Each bond of a square lattice is open with probability p = ½, the critical value. The cluster of interest spans the frame; eliminating its dangling bonds, and the clusters contained entirely inside it, leaves the backbone, with its conspicuous tremas. Kirkpatrick estimated D ≈ 1.6 for the backbone in the plane — "astonishingly close" to the gasket's log 3/log 2, and in space D ≈ 2.00 matches the skewed web below.

Gasket and carpet look alike but differ at the root. To disconnect a neighbourhood in a finite gasket, remove 3 or 4 points; in a carpet, the boundaries of any two holes never overlap, so whole intervals must be cut, and more of them the finer the approximation. Whyburn 1958: all such curves are topologically identical. In this finite-against-infinite dichotomy the gasket sides with the standard curves, the carpets with the whole plane — which is why a percolation backbone, cut by removing a few bonds, is modelled by a gasket and not a carpet. The distribution of the holes' sizes follows Nr(Λ > λ) ∝ F·λ−D, the rule known from the Cantor dust's gaps and Chapter 13's islands.

Plate 145, bottom

The Menger sponge

N = 20, r = 1/3 · D = log 20/log 3 ≈ 2.7268 · the triadic foam keeps 26 and has D ≈ 2.9656

Karl Menger's trema is a cross with spikes front and back: 7 of the 27 subcubes, leaving N = 20 — twelve "ropes" along the edges and eight "knots" on the corners. Both the curd and the whey are connected: water could flow between any two points of the holes, which is why Mandelbrot calls it a sponge. Removing only the centre subcube instead leaves a shell of 26 and gives the triadic fractal foam, where every trema is sealed off by infinitely many, infinitely thin layers — a reminder of the "space-time foam" of Wheeler and Hawking.

The book's photograph is reproduced from Blumenthal & Menger's Studies in Geometry; this figure is computed from the rule, drawn as the outer visible faces in three print-like tones, so it can be turned. Generalised sponges take a bigger base b: "light" sponges keep 12b − 16 cubes, "heavy" ones (odd b) keep b³ − 3b + 2; thick-wall foams give D = log(b³ − 1)/log b. Topologically the carpet is the universal plane curve, and the sponge the universal curve outright: the most complicated curve in any space.

Ramification without loops

N = 5, r = 1/3 · D = log 5/log 3 ≈ 1.4649

A variant of the Koch snowflake that achieves ramification through branches: the initiator is a square and the generator lifts the middle third onto a square tab. Every copy is placed as drawn. The teragons touch themselves — tab against tab — and the touching pieces merge, so the limit is a tree-like curve bristling with branch points: "totally different from the snowflake," as the mountain overleaf shows.

Plate 146

Split snowflake halls

eleven legs: six of 1/3, five of 1/(3√3) · 6·(1/3)^D + 5·(1/(3√3))^D = 1 · D ≈ 1.8687

Long ago and far away, the Great Ruler and his retinue had sat their power in the splendid Snowflake Halls. A schism occurs, a war follows, ending in stalemate, and finally Wise Elders draw a line to divide the Halls between the contenders from the North and the South. The line is the initiator [0, 1]; the division is this curve, grown from the same eleven-legged generator as the Monkeys Tree of Plate 31, and it splits the Koch snowflake of Plate 45 into two labyrinths.
