Self-similar and self-affine

B‍H: scale t by h, X by hH · graph D = 2 − H · zeroset D = 1 − H

A similarity shrinks every coordinate by the same ratio r; an affinity gives each coordinate its own ratio. The chapter's first entry fixes both words, because the graph of a Brown function can never be self-similar: time and height are different quantities. It is self-affine — take the piece over [0, h], stretch time by 1/h and height by 1/hH, and the result is statistically indistinguishable from the whole. That holds for exactly one exponent: the H the function was built with. The entry BROWN FRACTAL SETS catalogues these functions; H = 1/2 is ordinary Brownian motion, H ≠ 1/2 the fractional ones of chapter 27, persistent above 1/2 and antipersistent below.

Covering by balls, and the dimension in the jump

N(ρ) balls of radius ρ · log N(ρ)/log(1/ρ) → D · the d-measure jumps from ∞ to 0 at d = D

Every acceptable fractal dimension is metric, not topological: it comes from covering the set with balls of radius ρ. Counting the most economical cover gives N(ρ), and log N(ρ)/log(1/ρ) settles toward a limit as ρ shrinks. Hausdorff went further: weigh each ball by the test function h(ρ) = γ(d)ρd — the volume a ball of radius ρ would have in dimension d — and add. Besicovitch proved the striking law: for every set there is one critical D such that the total is infinite for every d < D and zero for every d > D. That jump is the Hausdorff–Besicovitch dimension, and "to a physicist, this definition means that D is a critical dimension."

Lévy stable random variables

s₁X₁ + s₂X₂ = sX with s₁D + s₂D = sD · long tails ∝ x−D−1 · closed forms only at Gauss, Cauchy and D = 1/2

The Gaussian is "scaling under addition": the sum of two independent copies is again Gaussian. Paul Lévy asked which other laws satisfy the same functional equation, s₁X₁ + s₂X₂ = sX, with the sizes balanced by s₁D + s₂D = sD, and found a family for every exponent 0 < D ≤ 2 and skewness −1 ≤ β ≤ 1. "Until my work, the stable variables were deemed 'pathological' or even 'monstrous'" — yet for D < 2 their long tails follow exactly the hyperbolic law of chapter 38, which is why they rule Lévy dusts and flights, cotton prices and the Holtsmark attraction of the stars. The second figure draws the entry's strangest consequence: given a large sum, the most likely split is not half-and-half but one addend taking nearly all of it.

Statistical analysis using R/S

Q(d) = R(d)/S(d) ∝ dJ · J = 1/2: no long-run dependence · J = H for fractional Gauss noise · robust when ⟨X²⟩ = ∞

Two assumptions were "a matter of course" in practical statistics: finite variance, and short-run dependence. Mandelbrot's case studies broke both, so correlations and spectra — which assume ⟨X²⟩ < ∞ — could not measure the long-run dependence he had injected to explain the Hurst phenomenon. His alternative is the rescaled range: over each span of length d, take the running sum, detrend it by its own bridge, and divide its range R by the span's standard deviation S. If R/S grows like dJ, the exponent J — the Hurst coefficient — reads off long-run dependence while staying "robust with respect to the marginal distribution": even a wild infinite-variance noise gives J = 1/2 as long as it has no memory.

Weierstrass functions and kin

W₀(t) = (1−w²)−1/2 Σ₀∞ wⁿ exp(2πibⁿt) · w = b−H · graph D = 2 − H, conjectured

Weierstrass's function — a sum of cosines at frequencies b, b², b³, … with amplitudes wⁿ = b−Hn — is continuous but nowhere differentiable. The entry retells both facts as spectral catastrophes. Differentiating multiplies each amplitude by its frequency, and the resulting energy diverges at high frequencies: the same ultraviolet catastrophe that killed blackbody theory around 1900, "the same divergence killed the old physics and the old mathematics". And the original series starts at frequency 1, which clashes with scaling at the low end; letting n run to −∞ cures it but diverges — the infrared catastrophe — unless one first subtracts W(0). The repaired W₁ is exactly self-affine about t = 0: squeeze time by b, shrink height by w, and the curve maps onto itself.

The other entries, briefly

a reader's map of the rest of the dictionary

Detrending and the Brown bridge Brown motion over [0, 2π] splits into a straight trend with random slope plus a Brown bridge pinned to equal endpoints — and only for Brown motion are the two independent. The entry warns that applied statisticians detrend samples of unknown origin as if this always worked: "this last implicit assumption is quite unwarranted."

Fractional integro-differentiation B‍H is the Riemann–Liouville fractional integral of order H − 1/2 of white noise: multiplying the Fourier transform by (1/f)H+1/2 deserves the name integration of fractional order. Integration smooths (persistence), differentiation roughens.

Fourier dimension and Salem sets How fast can the Fourier transform of a measure on a set decay? The best exponent defines a second dimension D‍F ≤ D, which also feels arithmetic: the triadic Cantor dust has D‍F = 0, while random dusts are typically Salem sets with D‍F = D. Since D‍F is easy to estimate from data, it can stand in for D.

On the definition of "fractal" Mandelbrot would rather have no definition — his 1975 essay had none. The 1977 one, D > D‍T, excludes sets he would prefer to include: the devil's staircase of Plate 83 "is intuitively a fractal", yet D = 1 = D‍T. "Lacking other criteria, I set the border in such a way as to achieve a short definition."

Similarity dimension: its pitfalls The set of trema endpoints of the Cantor dust is self-similar with the same N and r as the dust itself — but it is countable, so its Hausdorff–Besicovitch dimension is 0. Similarity dimension can overstate; adding the limit points restores agreement.

Scaling under truncation The hyperbolic distribution Pr(U > u) = u−D is the only one for which the rescaled tail "U/u₀, knowing U > u₀" does not depend on the cutoff u₀ — a two-line proof in the entry, and the reason truncated data keep looking the same.

Nonlacunar fractals Can a fractal meet every ball of space? Yes, but it must be a measure, not a closed set. De Wijs's ore model and Besicovitch's weighted curdling share mass unevenly among thirds forever: the limit is everywhere dense, with a concentrate of dimension D = −Σ π‍ᵢ log₃ π‍ᵢ — an entropy. This is the seed of what became multifractals.

Potentials, capacities, Frostman dimension The mass within radius R growing like RD ties D to the convergence of potential integrals: Frostman proved the Hausdorff–Besicovitch D is exactly the capacity dimension of potential theory. The entry uses it to restate why D = 1 pairs with Newton's 1/R kernel in the cosmological chapters.

Stationarity (degrees of) Mathematicians define it on the generating rules; practitioners "hold narrower views" and call any sample with a 1/fB spectrum, B > 0, nonstationary. The entry defends the gray zone — sporadic processes, conditional stationarity, weak against strong mixing — because the book's models live there.

Music: two properties of scaling Tempered scales place fundamentals at frequencies bⁿ — the spectrum of a Weierstrass function. And Voss & Clarke found the power and the instantaneous frequency of music from Bach to the Beatles are 1/f noises; random music triggered by a 1/f source sounds the most music-like.

Indicator and coindicator functions For a dust, the indicator function (1 on the set) is useless almost everywhere; Mandelbrot's coindicator is constant on each gap with independent random values — a function that carries the set's structure where analysis can reach it.

Dimensions that add and multiply Rules of thumb with precise hypotheses: products of independent sets add dimensions; intersections add codimensions E − D, so two Brown trails (D = 2) intersect in space E < 4 and miss for E ≥ 4; projections give min(E₀, D); subordination multiplies.
