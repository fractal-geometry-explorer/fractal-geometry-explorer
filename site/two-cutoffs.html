The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 4, "Variations and Disclaimers" · pages 20–24

Chapter 4Between Two Cutoffs

Chapter 4 is the book's chapter about itself — its style, its erudition, and above all its pictures: "in the theory of fractals, to see is to believe." It prints no figures, but it makes one geometric claim worth drawing: every illustration of a fractal is finite, so it can only be faithful between a large outer cutoff and a small inner cutoff — and that is no defect, because Nature's own fractals come with the same two cutoffs. The figures below put the claim to the test on the triadic Koch curve of chapter 6.

Elementary strokes in large but finite number

outer cutoff Ω = 1 (the initiator) · inner cutoff ε = 3⁻ᵏ · D = log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.2618 between them

Illustrating geometry "involves a basic fallacy": a line is unbounded and infinitely thin, while any drawing is of finite extent, positive thickness and rough edge. A self-similar curve adds a worse infinity — detail at every scale. Mandelbrot's answer is to cut it off at both ends: draw only the strokes longer than an inner cutoff ε, with a pen of real width, inside a frame that is the outer cutoff, and "the typical fractal curve can be evoked satisfactorily by elementary strokes in large but finite number." The ledger under the figure keeps the books: the fractal exists for you only where the drawing's range and the screen's range overlap. Zoom far enough in and the overlap empties — beneath ε there is only Euclid.

Chapter 4 names no particular curve, so using chapter 6's triadic Koch curve here is this page's choice, and the scale ledger is this page's own bookkeeping device — the book states the two cutoffs in words and draws nothing. The chapter adds that cutting off "is not only quite acceptable but even eminently appropriate, because both cutoffs are either present or suspected in Nature": a real coastline stops being self-similar somewhere below the pebble and somewhere above the continent.

"To see is to believe" — down to a pixel

stage k against stage k + 3 · ε shrinks 27-fold · difference counted pixel by pixel in the current view

What exactly does the eye certify when it believes a picture? Only the scales it can resolve. Here the same curve is drawn twice — carried to stage k, then three stages deeper, by which point every stroke has split into 64 — and the difference view paints every pixel where the two pictures disagree. While the inner cutoff stays above a pixel the disagreement is everywhere. Once it falls below, the count collapses, and no deeper stage will ever change the picture again: the eye is satisfied, and that is precisely why a finite drawing is enough. Zoom in and the lost detail comes back into range.

This is the same red-and-blue difference test this site uses to check its reconstructions against the book's scans, turned on the book's own argument. Pixels are compared at screen resolution in the current view, so the counts change as you pan and zoom; the thin disagreement that lingers even when ε is tiny is antialiasing along edges the two stages share. The chapter draws the practical conclusion for the whole book: since what the eye checks is a range of scales, graphics can arbitrate between model and reality — "when a chance mechanism agrees with the data from some analytic viewpoint but simulations of the model do not look at all 'real,' the analytic agreement should be suspect."
