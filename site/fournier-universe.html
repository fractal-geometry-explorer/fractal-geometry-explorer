The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 9, "Fractal View of Galaxy Clusters" · pages 84–96

Plates 95–96Fournier Universes

Galaxies gather into clusters, the clusters into superclusters, and no survey has found the scale where it stops. Mandelbrot reads the evidence as a fractal dust: the mass within distance R of us grows as M(R) ∝ RD with D well below 3, so the universe has no global density. The first fully described model is E. E. Fournier d'Albe's 1907 multiuniverse — worlds within worlds within worlds — which these two plates draw. Every figure is computed from the rules in the captions.

Plate 95

Projection of Fournier's multiuniverse

N = 7, 1/r = 7 · in space D = log 7/log 7 = 1 · on the page N = 5, D = log 5/log 7 ≈ 0.8270

Fournier's construction starts with a centered regular octahedron: four balls at the corners of a square whose diagonal is 12 units, one ball at the centre, and two more above and below the page at the same distance of 6. Each ball of radius 1 is a stellar aggregate of order 0. The smallest sphere holding all seven — radius 7 — is an aggregate of order 1; seven copies of it, placed the same way, make an aggregate of order 2, and so on without end. Each order multiplies the number of balls and the radius by the same 7, so the mass within radius R grows as M(R) ∝ R: dimension 1. The plate shows the order-3 aggregate to exact scale — at once its projection and its equatorial section, since the two polar balls of every aggregate hide exactly behind its centre dot.

Plate 96

A flat Fournier universe with D = 1

N = 5, 1/r = 5 · D = log 5/log 5 = 1 · carried one order further than Plate 95

Plate 95, "being drawn to exact scale, is not only hard to print and to see, but potentially misleading": the picture is not a universe of dimension 1 but its planar projection, of dimension log 5/log 7 ≈ 0.8270 < 1. Projecting flattens the polar balls into the centre dots, and the five that remain shrink by 7 while multiplying only by 5. To leave no wrong impression, the book re-runs the construction directly in the plane with 1/r = 5: now the five dots multiply exactly as fast as they shrink, and D = log 5/log 5 = 1. Because the pattern shrinks more slowly, it can be "carried one step further than is possible in Plate 95": four orders, 625 dots.

Chapter 9, pages 85–93

Mass, density and the dark night sky

M(R) ∝ RD · approximate density M(R)/(4/3)πR³ ∝ RD−3 → 0 · D < 2 keeps the night sky dark

Centre a sphere of radius R on Earth, weigh the mass M(R) inside, and let R grow. If the universe had a global density, M(R)/R³ would settle to it; de Vaucouleurs observed instead that the estimated density keeps falling, like RD−3 with D ≈ 1.23 — the signature of a fractal dust. Fournier argued D must be exactly 1: if M(R) ∝ R, the gravitational potential GM/R at the edge of every world ball is the same, so nowhere do stars fall in at anything near the speed of light — and indeed no star moves faster than c/300. Hoyle's 1953 theory of galaxy formation, a cascade of gas clouds collapsing and splitting into five at each stage, reaches D = 1 from the Jeans criterion by a different road.

The staircase is counted from the model itself: distances from the central ball to every other ball, out to the order the chart shows. It climbs along M = R and, between aggregate radii, sags down to M = R/7 — exactly the band the chapter describes ("for intermediate values of R, M(R) is smaller, reaching down to R/7"). The sky view backs the caption of Plate 95: a universe of dimension D < 2 projects onto a set of directions of zero area, so "in some directions the sky would appear quite black, although there is an infinite succession of universes" — the Blazing Sky Effect (often miscredited to Olbers) is exorcised geometrically, with no need to deny the clustering.
