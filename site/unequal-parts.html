Plates 56–57Unequal Parts

Koch's construction with one change: the generator's sides need not be equal. The curves become less relentlessly regular. Different parts of a curve need different numbers of stages to reach the same fine detail. The dimension now comes from an equation, Σ rmD = 1.

A Koch curve with unequal parts

Generator 1/4, 1/2, 1/2, 1/4 · D ≈ 1.4500

Plate 56 adds variety to the triadic Koch curve. The generator keeps Koch's shape (flat, up 60°, down 60°, flat), but the middle sides are twice as long as the outer ones: 1/4, 1/2, 1/2, 1/4. Solving Σ rmD = 1 gives D ≈ 1.4500, well above Koch's 1.2619 (the book prints 1.4490).

Mandelbrot continues each construction until the details reach a set small size. With equal ratios that takes a fixed number of stages. Here it doesn't: a piece reached through quarters gets small faster than one reached through halves.

Plates 56–57, caption

The dimension-generating function

G(D) = Σ rmD, and D is the root of G(D) = 1

To extend similarity dimension to unequal parts, Mandelbrot looks at ordinary shapes paved by pieces shrunk in ratios rm. An interval cut into unequal pieces needs Σ rm = 1, and a square paved by unequal squares needs Σ rm2 = 1. With N equal parts the old rule N rD = 1 reads Σ rmD = 1 too. So he forms G(D) = Σ rmD and takes D to be its unique real root of G(D) = 1. G is strictly decreasing, so there is exactly one.

Whether this D always equals the Hausdorff–Besicovitch dimension, he writes, "remains to be investigated. In every case I know of, it does." P. A. P. Moran had in fact proved it in 1946 for pieces that don't overlap; Hutchinson's 1981 theory of self-similar sets made that condition standard.

Plate 57, top

A row of trees, just below D = 2

Two legs, r ≈ 0.556 and 0.808 · D ≈ 1.8797

The generator is just two legs of unequal length meeting at a little more than a right angle. Every copy is flipped to the inside. The land that survives between the copies grows into trunks and branches. Mandelbrot says the resemblance to a row of trees "is not accidental" (Chapter 17). As the angle closes to 90°, D → 2 and the coastline tends to the Peano–Pólya curve, which fills the whole triangle (Chapter 7).

Because every copy is flipped, the stages take turns: odd stages add land and even stages carve it away. Stopping pieces at different stages mixes the two into dark blotches; ending every piece on an even, carving stage gives the plate's clean trees. At exactly 90° the legs satisfy r₁² + r₂² = 1. The two flipped copies then tile the triangle with nothing left over, D = 2, and the curve fills the triangle.

Plate 57, bottom

D slightly above 1

Four flats and two small bumps · D ≈ 1.054

At the other extreme, the curve at the bottom of Plate 57 is almost straight. Its generator, reconstructed from the figure, has four flat sides of 0.225, with one small 60° bump of side 0.05 pointing up and one pointing down between them. The same bumps then appear at every scale, ever smaller, up and down along the line. Each stage adds only about a tenth to the length, so D is only just above 1.

The printed figure is cropped. It stops partway through a small triangle on the right and trims a sliver off the left. Completed, the curve is symmetric: turning it half a turn about its centre swaps every up bump with a down bump. The bump positions in the book fit that exactly.
