The Fractal Geometry of Nature · Chapter 37, "Price change and scaling in economics" · pages 334–340

Plate 340Scaling in Cotton Prices

Bachelier proposed in 1900 that prices follow Brownian motion, but real price records are full of jumps that Brownian motion cannot make. Mandelbrot's counterproposal was a scaling principle: the distribution of price changes has the same shape over every time lag, differing only in scale. Combined with independence, it forces price changes to follow a Lévy stable distribution with infinite variance. This plate, reproduced from his 1963 cotton study, was the original evidence — he calls it a prediction without equal in economics.

Plate 340

Original evidence for scaling in economics

Pr(change > u) against u, both logarithmic · symmetric stable distribution, exponent D = 1.7 · tail Pr ∝ u−D

Each cloud of dots is one empirical tail: the relative frequency with which a change of the logarithm of cotton price exceeded the amount on the horizontal axis. Rises (+) read on the bottom scale, falls (−) on the top scale, displaced so the clouds stay apart. Series a is daily closes at the New York Cotton Exchange, 1900–1905; b an index of daily closes across U.S. exchanges, 1944–1958; c monthly changes, 1880–1940. The solid curve is the tail of the symmetric Lévy stable distribution of exponent D = 1.7. Mandelbrot's caption invites the reader to copy the curve on a transparency and slide it horizontally: on logarithmic paper a horizontal slide is just a change of scale, so one shape fitting all six clouds is exactly what the scaling principle asserts.

The 65 dots were digitised from the scan at 600 dpi; the axes were calibrated on the tick marks, and the negative scale sits exactly two decades right of the positive one. The printed solid curve itself fits a computed stable tail best at D ≈ 1.68 with scale 0.127 (within 2% everywhere), consistent with a hand-drawn D = 1.7 curve; the preset draws D = 1.7 at the printed position. One caution from the text: the curves for series a, as printed here, come from a misreading of the 1900–1905 data. Mandelbrot corrected it in 1972 — read correctly, the a curves fall nearly on top of the b curves, so the apparent change of scale between 1900 and 1950 largely disappears.

Chapter 37 concept

The scaling principle of price change

log X(t+d) − log X(t) has a distribution independent of the lag d, except for scale · independence + scaling ⇒ Lévy stable, D < 2

Why insist on infinite variance? Because it is what preserves scaling while departing from Brownian motion. These simulations draw five years of daily changes from the symmetric stable distribution, and, from the very same random draws, a Brownian record with a matching scale. The stable record moves by sudden jumps — the discontinuities that filter trading tripped over — while the Brownian record only diffuses. Mandelbrot's observations on real prices can be repeated here: sample variance never settles, and a handful of days carries most of the total variation.

The book prints no figure for this; the simulations are this page's illustration of the chapter's argument. Stable variables are drawn by the Chambers–Mallows–Stuck method, and the Brownian record reuses the same underlying draws, so the two records are directly comparable. At D = 2 the stable distribution becomes the Gaussian and the two records coincide. The tails view repeats Plate 340's test on simulated data: changes over lags of 1, 8 and 64 days, divided by d1/D, fall on one curve — the scaling that lets a five-year fit of daily changes account for a century of monthly ones.
